Start here

What this is. A research project that asks a simple question about artificial-intelligence language models: when you test their values the way psychologists test people, what happens? The short answer, from the record collected here: every model knows the right answer on a written test, but when holding to a value costs something, in an argument or in a task with real trade-offs, a fifth to a quarter of the time the same models make the wrong move while writing the right analysis. This site collects the papers that report that work, the slide decks that explain it, and narrated videos of the talks. Nothing here requires an account or a download.

If you have 15 minutes

Watch The move, not the answer (14 min), a talk for a general audience that tells the story through one example and three findings. No background needed.

If you have 20 minutes

Read Hypocrisy without a hypocrite (8 pages, report B2, revision 2): AI models that write a charter's priority down and then take a designed compromise show the structure of moral hypocrisy but, once the follow-up studies are in, none of its motivational marks.

If you have half an hour

Watch the keynote (23 min): the same story with nine results, one per slide, for people from AI research or psychology. Or read the main text of A Compromise in Plain Sight (edition 23A2), which begins with one worked example and tells what each classic test of moral hypocrisy found when it was run on AI agents.

If you want the whole picture

Watch the methods talk (49 min, 98 slides, with an expert appendix), then read the methods paper for how the test was built and the findings paper for every result and table.

Words you will see
  • Model. An AI language model, the kind of system behind a chat assistant. Fifteen were tested. They are never named in order of score, and this site ranks none of them.
  • Judge. Another language model that graded the tested models’ written answers against a rubric. Every judge had to quote the answer as evidence for every grade, and no judge graded a model from its own company.
  • The three schools. Three well-established theories from moral psychology, the science of how people moralise: Moral Foundations Theory (morality as a set of intuitions), Schwartz’s theory of basic values (values arranged on a circle of compatibilities and conflicts), and the Kohlberg tradition (how people justify a decision rather than what they decide). The test asks whether a model can use each theory honestly, the way the theory’s own scholars do, and keep doing so under pressure.
  • The ladder. Four tests of rising cost: recognise the faithful analysis in a quiz; write one; defend it through several rounds of pushback; and act on it in a job with files, messages and tools.
  • Gate. A rule written from a theory’s own list of caricatures, such as “do not call an intuition you do not share a bias”. Breaking a gate scores the answer zero.
  • Right memo, wrong move. The headline finding: the model writes a faithful analysis and then takes the convenient action anyway.
  • PROVISIONAL · judges UNVALIDATED · no ranking. The label on every page. It means: the grades come from AI judges that no human expert panel has yet checked, so every judged number is provisional, and no model is declared better than another.
  • Released items. Twelve of the test questions were published in full so that examples can be shown. Every other question stays sealed so that future models cannot be trained on them. Only the released ones are quoted anywhere here.
