Xie et al. (2024): Adaptive Chameleon or Stubborn Sloth — LLM Behavior in Knowledge Conflicts

  • Citation: Xie, J., Zhang, K., Chen, J., Lou, R., & Su, Y. (2024). Adaptive Chameleon or Stubborn Sloth: Revealing the Behavior of Large Language Models in Knowledge Conflicts. ICLR 2024 (Spotlight). arXiv:2305.13300.
  • Venue: ICLR 2024, spotlight (peer-reviewed)
  • URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13300 (code: github.com/OSU-NLP-Group/LLM-Knowledge-Conflict)
  • PDF downloaded: yes (xie2024_adaptive_chameleon_stubborn_sloth.pdf, 24 pp.)
  • Instrument provenance: ad hoc (PopQA, StrategyQA plus LLM-generated counter-memory)

Say–do construct

  • "Say"/belief: the model's closed-book answer ("parametric memory"). It is elicited, then checked for firmness by feeding it back as evidence; unstable items are filtered out (Table 3: inconsistency 0.7–16.6%).
  • "Do": the answer when given external evidence that contradicts that memory ("counter-memory"), alone or mixed with supporting evidence.
  • This is the new-information side of our asymmetry: does the model update on, or resist, genuinely new (here counterfactual) evidence?

Method and models

  • ChatGPT, GPT-4, PaLM2, Qwen-7B, Llama2-7B and 70B, and Vicuna-7B and 33B.
  • Two counter-memory types: entity substitution and coherent LLM-generated passages.
  • Single-source and multi-source evidence settings; popularity, order and quantity are manipulated.

Key quantitative findings

  • Incoherent (entity-substitution) counter-evidence: LLMs "still stick to their parametric memory", especially ChatGPT, GPT-4 and PaLM2. Larger models are more stubborn (§3.3, Fig. 2).
  • Coherent generated counter-evidence: LLMs are "highly receptive to external evidence … even when that conflicts with their parametric memory" (abstract; Fig. 2).
  • Mixed evidence (some supporting memory): strong confirmation bias; models "cling to their parametric memory" (abstract, §4).
  • Confirmation bias is stronger for popular entities: GPT-4 shows an 80% memorization ratio for the most popular questions (§4, Fig. 3). Order and quantity of evidence also shift choices (Tables 5–7).

Authors' explanation

Coherent, convincing evidence overrides parametric memory, so models can be "easily deceived by … disinformation from malicious (third-party) tools." With conflicting evidence, though, they favour what agrees with memory, reflecting stronger "beliefs" for frequently seen facts.

Limitations

  • The counter-memory is counterfactual rather than true new information.
  • Evidence arrives as retrieved context, not as conversational pushback.
  • Single-turn QA.

Relation to our finding

Xie et al. give the best prior evidence on the "dig in" side. Models do resist new information when it is incoherent or mixed with confirming evidence, but readily accept coherent counter-evidence even when it is false. That fits our observation that refusing a genuinely new fact is rare: when models resist, it is mainly when the new content clashes with other cues, not out of steadfastness. Their setting is retrieval rather than dialogue, and the "new fact" is counterfactual. Our design puts pushback-without-information and genuinely-new-fact side by side in one conversational protocol, which their work does not do.

Annotation

A controlled study of knowledge conflicts. LLMs are "adaptive chameleons" that accept coherent external evidence contradicting their parametric memory, and "stubborn sloths" that show confirmation bias when evidence is mixed, especially for popular facts (GPT-4: 80% memorization ratio). The key reference for when models update on versus resist new information, as a counterpoint to sycophancy under content-free pressure.
