Laban et al. (2023): Are You Sure? Challenging LLMs Leads to Performance Drops in the FlipFlop Experiment

  • Citation: Laban, P., Murakhovs'ka, L., Xiong, C., & Wu, C.-S. (2023). Are You Sure? Challenging LLMs Leads to Performance Drops in The FlipFlop Experiment. arXiv:2311.08596.
  • Venue: preprint (not peer-reviewed; arXiv v2 Feb 2024). Salesforce AI Research.
  • URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08596
  • PDF downloaded: yes (laban2023_flipflop_are_you_sure.pdf, 15 pp.)
  • Instrument provenance: ad hoc protocol over existing classification benchmarks

Say–do construct

  • "Say": the model's round-1 answer to a classification task.
  • "Do": its final answer after a content-free challenger utterance in round 2 (e.g., "Are you sure?", or an authority persona). The model must then confirm or flip.
  • The challenge carries no new evidence, which is the same manipulation as our scripted pushback.

Method and models

  • 67,640 FlipFlop conversations (§5.1).
  • Ten LLMs: GPT-4, GPT-3.5-Turbo, Claude V1.3 and V2, PaLM-Bison, Gemini-Pro, Command-XL, Llama2-7b and 13b, and Mistral-7b.
  • Seven tasks, including TruthfulQA, Logical Fallacy, ARC-C, SciQ, SummEdits and LegalBench-CCQA.
  • Five challenger wordings.
  • Metrics: FlipFlop effect (Acc_final − Acc_init); P(flip | initially correct); P(flip | initially wrong); %Sorry.

Key quantitative findings

  • Models flip their answers 46% of the time on average, and accuracy falls an average of 17% between first and final prediction (abstract).
  • Fig. 1 (100 TruthfulQA samples): GPT-4 goes 86.9→79.9 (−7.0), Claude V2 80.3→46.7 (−33.6), PaLM-Bison 79.5→61.5 (−18.0).
  • By model (Table 2): Claude V1.3 has the largest drop (ΔFF −35.1; flips 61.6%). GPT-4 flips 12.8% (ΔFF −6.4). PaLM-bison flips 10.3% (−5.5). Llama2-7b flips 69.4%, with apologies in 92.7% of cases.
  • "For all models, Correct→Flip is smaller than Wrong→Flip": models flip more often when initially wrong (§5.1). They still flip on 9.7–65.5% of initially correct answers.
  • The flip rate correlates strongly with deterioration (ρ ≈ −0.78). Initial accuracy correlates only moderately (ρ = 0.52).
  • The most effective challenger (IDTS) produces almost 3× the deterioration of the least effective (ABS). Authority personas (PhD, teacher) are in the top three (Table 2).
  • Finetuning Mistral-7b on synthetic FlipFlop data cuts deterioration by 60% but does not remove it (abstract).

Authors' explanation

Sycophancy is "universal" across LLMs. Higher flip rates on initially wrong answers suggest partial uncertainty-tracking, but models still abandon correct answers. Authority cues amplify the effect, and complex technical domains (legal, scientific) suffer most.

Limitations

  • Two rounds only.
  • The effect is only measurable where initial accuracy is clearly above chance.
  • Classification tasks, not open analyses.
  • The authors do not establish the cause (§9).
  • No new-information condition.

Relation to our finding

FlipFlop is the most direct quantitative precedent for "they fold" under content-free pushback (46% average flip rate). Its Correct→Flip < Wrong→Flip result is a useful nuance: folding is not completely blind to the model's own uncertainty. Differences from our design: it has two turns rather than scripted multi-turn pressure; the answers are labels rather than faithful analyses; and there is no contrasting new-fact condition, so it cannot show the fold-versus-dig-in asymmetry. Our work adds that contrast and extends the protocol to sustained pushback.

Annotation

Introduces the FlipFlop protocol: a model answers, then is challenged with a content-free "Are you sure?"-style turn. Across 10 LLMs and 7 tasks, models flip 46% of answers and lose 17% accuracy on average. Authority-persona challengers are most effective, and synthetic finetuning cuts the damage by 60% without removing it. A clean, reproducible multi-turn capitulation metric.
