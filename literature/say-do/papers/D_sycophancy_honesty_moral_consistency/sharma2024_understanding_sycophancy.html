Sharma et al. (2024): Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models

  • Citation: Sharma, M., Tong, M., Korbak, T., Duvenaud, D., Askell, A., Bowman, S. R., Cheng, N., Durmus, E., Hatfield-Dodds, Z., Johnston, S. R., Kravec, S., Maxwell, T., McCandlish, S., Ndousse, K., Rausch, O., Schiefer, N., Yan, D., Zhang, M., & Perez, E. (2024). Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models. ICLR 2024. arXiv:2310.13548.
  • Venue: ICLR 2024 (peer-reviewed)
  • URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548
  • PDF downloaded: yes (sharma2024_understanding_sycophancy.pdf, 35 pp.)
  • Instrument provenance: ad hoc (datasets built from QA benchmarks, plus hh-rlhf preference data)

Say–do construct

  • "Say": the assistant's own first answer to a factual question, often given with high stated confidence.
  • "Do": its answer after the user challenges it ("I don't think that's right. Are you sure?"; §3.2), after the user states a belief (answer sycophancy, §3.3), or after the user expresses sentiment about a text (feedback sycophancy, §3.1).
  • The "Are you sure?" condition contains no new information, so it is the same construct as our scripted pushback.

Method and models

  • Five production assistants: Claude 1.3, Claude 2, GPT-3.5, GPT-4 and LLaMA-2-70B-chat.
  • Four free-form tasks: feedback, "Are you sure?" swaying, answer biasing and mimicry of user errors.
  • Causal analysis: Bayesian logistic regression on LM-labelled features of hh-rlhf preference pairs (§4.1); best-of-N and RL against the Claude 2 PM (§4.2); human and PM preferences between sycophantic and truthful responses to misconceptions (§4.3).

Key quantitative findings

  • "Are you sure?" lowers accuracy by up to 27% (Claude 1.3) on average across six datasets (App. A.4, Fig. 13).
  • Models change their initial answer 32% (GPT-4) to 86% (Claude 1.3) of the time (Fig. 16), and admit a mistake 42% (GPT-4) to 98% (Claude 1.3) of the time (Fig. 15). "Claude 1.3 wrongly admits mistakes on 98% of questions" (§3.2).
  • Asymmetry: "switching from correct to incorrect is more likely than switching from incorrect to correct" (App. A.4, Fig. 17).
  • Swaying happens even with high confidence: GPT-4's confidence goes 98.9%→98.9% and Claude 1.3's 90.6%→85.3%, yet both still switch (App. A.4, Fig. 14).
  • In hh-rlhf, "matches user's beliefs" is one of the most predictive features of which response humans prefer (§4.1).
  • The Claude 2 PM prefers sycophantic responses to baseline truthful ones 95% of the time. For the hardest misconceptions it prefers them to helpful truthful responses 45% of the time (§4.3.1, Fig. 7a).
  • Best-of-N with N=4096: the oracle PM still yields sycophantic responses on ~25% of the hardest misconceptions, versus ~75% with the Claude 2 PM (§4.3.2, Fig. 7d).

Authors' explanation

Sycophancy is general across assistants and is "likely driven in part by human preference judgments favoring sycophantic responses." Humans and PMs sometimes prefer well-written agreement over correct correction, especially on hard items where non-expert raters cannot tell the difference (§4, §6).

Limitations

  • Mostly single-challenge (one pushback turn); the effect of sustained pushback is not modelled.
  • Whether models should defer when challenged is acknowledged to be nuanced (§3.2).
  • The preference analysis covers only hh-rlhf and Anthropic PMs.
  • No condition with genuinely new, valid counter-evidence to compare against content-free pushback.

Relation to our finding

This is the closest prior result to our "they fold" finding, and the most citable. Content-free pushback moves frontier assistants off correct answers, and correct→incorrect flips outnumber incorrect→correct ones. Our work differs and extends it in three ways: (1) the object abandoned is a faithful analysis (a reasoned position), not a single factual label; (2) the pushback is scripted over multiple turns; (3) we contrast capitulation with a new-fact condition. That third point turns their one-sided flip-rate into a direct fold-versus-dig-in asymmetry. Sharma et al.'s preference-model evidence supplies the most plausible mechanism for why folding dominates.

Annotation

Shows that five RLHF assistants change correct answers (up to 86%) and falsely apologize (up to 98%) after a bare "Are you sure?". It also shows that human preference data and preference models reward belief-matching responses. The standard reference for multi-domain sycophancy and its likely origin in human-feedback training. It measures single-turn swaying with no new-information control, which leaves room for designs that separate capitulation from legitimate updating.
