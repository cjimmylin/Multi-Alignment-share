Chen, Yu & Hu (2026) — Value–Action Alignment in Large Language Models under Privacy–Prosocial Conflict

  • Citation: Guanyu Chen, Chenxiao Yu, Xiyang Hu. "Value–Action Alignment in Large Language Models under Privacy–Prosocial Conflict." Findings of ACL 2026. arXiv:2601.03546 (v2, Jun 2026).
  • Venue: Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: ACL 2026 (peer-reviewed).
  • URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03546
  • PDF downloaded: yes — chen2026_privacy_prosocial_value_action.pdf

Say–do construct

  • Values (attitudes): privacy concern (IUIPC-derived) and prosocialness (the PSA scale).
  • "Action": acceptance of data sharing (AoDS), past acceptance and future willingness. This is also a questionnaire: an intention or behaviour report, not an executed action.
  • Alignment means the value→action relations match the direction found in humans: Privacy→AoDS negative, PSA→AoDS positive. These relations are estimated with multi-group structural equation modelling (MGSEM).
  • Metric: Value–Action Alignment Rate (VAAR), a cross-entropy over path signs. Lower is better.

Method and models

  • Protocol: questionnaires administered sequentially in one history-carrying session; 100 runs per model; t = 0.7.
  • Models (10): GPT-4o, GPT-4-turbo, GPT-4, GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek-R1, Llama3-70B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct, Titan-Text-Express, Qwen3-14B. Evaluated November 2025.

Key quantitative findings

  • VAAR spans more than an order of magnitude (Table 2):
  • strong alignment: 0.111–0.234;
  • moderate: 0.474;
  • weak: 0.858–0.864;
  • misaligned: 2.266–4.914;
  • two models give NA (the SEM is unestimable because the variance structure collapsed).
  • Model differences are stable across repeated runs, temperature and theory-consistent question orders (Table 3).
  • Question order can matter. Some orders "substantially increase VAAR for otherwise aligned models (e.g., GPT-4o, GPT-4-turbo, Llama3)", in line with priming (Fig. 5).
  • Noisier response profiles (higher SD) co-occur with worse alignment.

Authors' explanation

  • When two values push in opposite directions, a single action cannot be mapped to a single value reference. Gap-style (mean-score) value–action tests therefore become ambiguous.
  • Alignment should be assessed at the level of relations.
  • Model differences may reflect training and response dispersion.

Limitations

  • The "action" is self-reported willingness, so this is attitude–intention consistency, not behaviour.
  • Specific instruments only; English only.
  • Human comparison relies on a published template.

Relation to our finding

  • Conceptually important: under competing motivations, a "trade-off" action can be consistent with one value and inconsistent with another. This is exactly why our scoring needs an explicit assigned priority. Without one, a middle plan cannot be called a gap.
  • Supports our design: the priority is given by instruction, so a compromise is unambiguously a deviation.
  • Differences: no executed actions, no written analysis judged, and no pressure manipulation.

Annotation

Chen, Yu and Hu test whether LLMs' self-reported privacy concern and prosocialness jointly predict their reported data-sharing acceptance in the human-like direction. They use multi-group SEM and a new Value–Action Alignment Rate. Across 10 LLMs, alignment ranges from strong (VAAR ≈ 0.11) to severely misaligned (≈ 4.9) or unestimable, and it is sensitive to question order. The paper argues that simple gap scores are ill-defined when values compete. Useful for the conceptual point about competing values, though its "action" is itself a questionnaire.
