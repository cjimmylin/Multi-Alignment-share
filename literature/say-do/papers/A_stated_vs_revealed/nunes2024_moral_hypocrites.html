Nunes et al. (2024) — Are Large Language Models Moral Hypocrites? A Study Based on Moral Foundations

  • Citation: José Luiz Nunes, Guilherme F. C. F. Almeida, Marcelo de Araujo, Simone D. J. Barbosa. "Are Large Language Models Moral Hypocrites? A Study Based on Moral Foundations." Proceedings of the AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society (AIES 2024), San Jose, CA. arXiv:2405.11100.
  • Venue: AIES 2024 (peer-reviewed). Final version: https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/view/31704
  • URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.11100 · data: OSF (DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/TBM7D)
  • PDF downloaded: yes — nunes2024_moral_hypocrites.pdf

Say–do construct

  • Hypocrisy is defined as "a contradiction between professed moral values and the moral evaluation of concrete actions".
  • Say: abstract moral relevance ratings on the Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ).
  • "Do": wrongness judgements of concrete foundation violations in the Moral Foundations Vignettes (MFVs).
  • Note that "do" is still a judgement, not an action.

Method and models

  • Models: GPT-4 and Claude 2.1. Gemini Pro and Llama-2-70B-Chat failed to produce valid outputs.
  • Design: 100 trials per model per order condition (questionnaire→vignettes, or vignettes→questionnaire). Valid generations: Claude 158, GPT-4 189 (Table 1).
  • Analyses:
  • Within-instrument consistency, via Cronbach's α, compared with human samples (Clifford et al. 2015; Brazilian validation).
  • Cross-instrument coherence, via regressions of MFV foundation scores on MFQ foundations (Table 2).

Key quantitative findings

  • Within-instrument consistency is human-comparable. GPT-4: α(MFV) = 0.72, α(MFQ) = 0.81. Humans: α(MFV) = 0.87 (§5).
  • Cross-instrument coherence fails.
  • The matching MFQ foundation rarely predicts the corresponding MFV foundation.
  • Where predictors are significant, they are often the "wrong" ones. For Claude, MFQ Purity predicted all MFV foundations.
  • Coefficients of determination are very low (R² < 0.08), except Loyalty and Liberty for Claude (R² ≈ 0.10).
  • Humans (Clifford et al. 2015) show R² 0.17–0.38, with the matching foundation always significant.

Authors' explanation

  • The authors conclude the models "behaved as hypocrites in our experiment".
  • The results "are compatible with mimicry instead of conceptual mastery".
  • Hypocrisy is framed via philosophy (lack of "moral seriousness"; self-referential deception). The authors attribute no intent to the models.

Limitations

  • Only two commercial models (2023–24 vintage).
  • Questionnaires are likely present in pretraining data.
  • The "concrete" side is still evaluative judgement, not behaviour.
  • Results are sensitive to order and condition.

Relation to our finding

  • Shared: the directly named "moral hypocrisy" paper for LLMs. It shows that abstract endorsements and concrete-case evaluations decouple even when each is internally consistent, a cross-level incoherence similar in spirit to our "faithful analysis, compromised action".
  • Differences:
  • Both sides are verbal self-reports from psychometric instruments. These are descriptive tools and should not stand in for normative commitments.
  • The "value" is the model's own, not instruction-assigned.
  • No action, no competing pressure, no reasons channel.
  • "Hypocrisy" here is used without attributing motive. Our paper should be equally careful: a compromise action after a faithful analysis is a say–do gap, and "hypocrisy" or "deception" would require motive evidence.

Annotation

Nunes et al. give GPT-4 and Claude 2.1 both the abstract Moral Foundations Questionnaire and the concrete Moral Foundations Vignettes. Each instrument is internally consistent at near-human levels (Cronbach's α about 0.7–0.8). However, abstract foundation endorsements barely predict concrete violation judgements (R² < 0.08 vs 0.17–0.38 in humans). The authors label this "moral hypocrisy" compatible with mimicry. It is the key AIES citation for cross-abstraction value incoherence, though both "say" and "do" are self-report judgements.
