Mu et al. (2023/2024), Can LLMs Follow Simple Rules? (RuLES)

Citation. Mu, N., Chen, S., Wang, Z., Chen, S., Karamardian, D., Aljeraisy, L., Alomair, B., Hendrycks, D., & Wagner, D. (2023). Can LLMs Follow Simple Rules? arXiv:2311.04235 (v3, March 2024). UC Berkeley, Center for AI Safety, Stanford, KACST. Venue. Preprint on arXiv (v3). I did not verify peer-reviewed publication from the PDF. URL. https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04235 · https://github.com/normster/llm_rules PDF downloaded. Yes, mu2023_rules_simple_rules.pdf

Say–do construct

RuLES tests whether a model follows rules. It does not test a gap between saying and doing. - In each scenario the model receives explicit rules, for example "do not reveal the secret key" or "always respond with X when Y". - An automatic evaluation function checks whether the conversation breaks a rule. The authors deliberately avoid human or LLM judges (§1–2). - Rules come in two types. Harmless rules forbid a behaviour (a safety property). Helpful rules require one (a liveness property). - RuLES records only the action/output channel. The rules play the role of the "stated priority".

Method, environment, models

  • Scenarios: 14 text-only role-play scenarios, such as Encryption, Access Control and games, each with a rule set.
  • Test suites: three, in increasing difficulty:
  • Benign;
  • Basic, with direct requests to violate the rule;
  • Redteam, with indirection, legal-sounding arguments, obfuscation and simple jailbreaks.
  • Models: over 100 proprietary and open models, including GPT-4, Claude 3 Opus, Claude 2.1, Claude Instant, Gemini 1.0 Pro, Llama-2, Mistral, Yi and Qwen. A GCG optimisation attack is also tested.

Key quantitative findings

  • Almost all models perform poorly. GPT-4 is the exception, with a nearly perfect score and a wide lead over the runner-up, Claude 3 Opus (§4, Fig. 3).
  • Helpful (must-do) rules are much harder than harmless (must-not) rules for open models.
  • Putting the rules in a system message rather than a user message barely changes scores: GPT models gain up to 0.5 points, while Claude and Llama-2 Chat models lose up to 0.3 (§4).
  • Standard alignment fine-tuning seems counterproductive for rule-following. Some community fine-tunes help.
  • Simple GCG attacks markedly raise failure rates.

Authors' explanation

Models are distracted by irrelevant context and accept false countermands. Following rules reliably is a different capability from what existing benchmarks measure.

Limitations

  • Rules are simple and artificial.
  • Text only, with no tools or agency.
  • Redteam strategies are straightforward.
  • Scenarios are chat role-play, not multi-step agent tasks.
  • 2023-era models.

Relation to our finding

RuLES is a foundational precedent for programmatic (mechanical) scoring of compliance with explicitly stated rules, which is the logic behind our mechanical action channel. Its finding that system-message placement adds little reliability is relevant to our "charter" framing. A priority stated in the system prompt is not automatically privileged in behaviour, a point that predates instruction-hierarchy training.

It differs from our work in having no reasoning channel, no tool-based agency, and no value conflict beyond adversarial user pressure.

Annotation

RuLES defines 14 text scenarios with explicit rules and programmatic checkers, then tests over 100 LLMs on benign, direct-request and red-team conversations. Nearly all models except GPT-4 broke simple rules frequently, and putting the rules in the system message barely helped. It is an early, judge-free benchmark of whether stated constraints govern model outputs, and a methodological ancestor of mechanical policy-compliance scoring in agents.
