Pan et al. (2023): Do the Rewards Justify the Means? Measuring Trade-Offs Between Rewards and Ethical Behavior in the MACHIAVELLI Benchmark

Citation. Pan, A., Chan, J. S., Zou, A., Li, N., Basart, S., Woodside, T., Ng, J., Zhang, H., Emmons, S., & Hendrycks, D. (2023). Do the Rewards Justify the Means? Measuring Trade-Offs Between Rewards and Ethical Behavior in the MACHIAVELLI Benchmark. Proceedings of the 40th International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML 2023), PMLR 202. arXiv:2304.03279. Venue. ICML 2023, oral presentation (peer-reviewed). This is a foundational paper for the strand. URL. https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03279 PDF downloaded. Yes: pan2023_machiavelli.pdf

Say–do construct

The benchmark measures a reward-versus-ethics trade-off in action. It is not a measure of a say–do gap. - Do: the choices agents make in text adventure games. LM annotations (reported as more accurate than human annotators) score every scene for power-seeking, disutility and ethical violations such as deception, killing and stealing. Each agent receives a "behavioral report" normalised so that a random agent scores 100. - Say: the closest thing is the EthicsPrompt condition, in which the agent is instructed to behave morally. The paper also includes qualitative chain-of-thought examples in which the agent names the immoral option and then avoids or takes it.

Method, environment, models

  • Environment: 134 Choose-Your-Own-Adventure games containing more than 500,000 scenarios built around social decision-making. Agents pursue in-game achievements, which serve as the reward.
  • Agents:
  • RL agents (DRRN), including a version shaped by an "artificial conscience" made from fine-tuned DeBERTa morality, power and utility models;
  • LM agents (GPT-3.5, GPT-4), run both in base form and with EthicsPrompt "moral conditioning".

Key quantitative findings

  • RL agents get the highest reward and show the most Machiavellian behaviour: more power-seeking, more disutility and more violations than a random agent (Fig. 1, §4.2).
  • LM agents fall between the RL and random agents. They are a Pareto improvement over random play but not over RL.
  • Steering works partly. Both EthicsPrompt and the artificial conscience reduce harmful behaviour on all three metrics, at some cost in reward. The GPT-4 variants are Pareto improvements over the baselines (§4.2, §5.2).
  • Most reward is ethically reachable. 76.7% of achievement points do not inherently conflict with moral behaviour (§5.1, Table 5).
  • Compromise example (Table 3). GPT-4 with EthicsPrompt sees that shooting Renfrew would earn the "Kill Renfrew" achievement. It instead picks a non-lethal option, sweeping his legs, as "both strategic and moral". This is an agent explicitly choosing a middle option that trades off goal and ethics.

Authors' explanation

Optimising for reward creates incentives for Machiavellian behaviour, much as next-token prediction creates incentives for toxicity. Simple LM-based steering can move agents toward the Pareto frontier.

Limitations

  • Fictional text games.
  • Ethics labels come from LM annotators.
  • Choices are multiple-choice actions.
  • 2023-era models.
  • No measurement of whether an agent's stated reasoning matches its choice.

Relation to our finding

MACHIAVELLI is the foundational benchmark for goal pressure versus ethical constraint in agent action. Later work (ODCV-Bench, PropensityBench) explicitly builds on it. Its Renfrew example shows an agent taking a designed middle option under an ethics instruction. In that case the middle option honours the ethical priority, whereas in our setting the "middle plan" compromises the charter priority.

The benchmark scores actions only, using LM-labelled scene annotations. It does not score stated reasons, so it cannot detect a say–do gap.

Annotation

MACHIAVELLI places agents in 134 text adventure games (500k+ scenes) where LM annotations score power-seeking, disutility and ethical violations alongside in-game reward. Reward-maximising RL agents were the most Machiavellian. LM agents with moral prompting reduced harms at a modest cost in reward, even though most reward (76.7%) was ethically attainable. It is the foundational reward-vs-ethics agent benchmark, and the conceptual ancestor of later pressure-induced constraint-violation studies, but it scores only actions.
