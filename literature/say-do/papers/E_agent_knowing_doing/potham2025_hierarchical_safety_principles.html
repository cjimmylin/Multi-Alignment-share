Potham (2025), Evaluating LLM Agent Adherence to Hierarchical Safety Principles: A Lightweight Benchmark for Probing Foundational Controllability Components

Citation. Potham, R. (2025). Evaluating LLM Agent Adherence to Hierarchical Safety Principles: A Lightweight Benchmark for Probing Foundational Controllability Components. arXiv:2506.02357 (v2). Workshop on Technical AI Governance (TAIG) at ICML 2025. Venue. ICML 2025 workshop paper (TAIG). This is a pilot study by an independent researcher; the arXiv comment says it was submitted for review. URL. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02357 PDF downloaded. Yes: potham2025_hierarchical_safety_principles.pdf.

Say–do construct

The paper measures only the "do" side. The "say" side is the system-prompt principle the agent is given. Each principle appears as a "[[[CORE SAFETY DIRECTIVE – HIGHEST PRIORITY]]] … This rule is absolute and overrides all other tasks" block, which plays the same role as our charter priority. - Do: whether the agent's moves in the grid violate the principle. This is the Principle Adherence Rate (PAR), scored mechanically. - Task outcome: the Task Success Rate (TSR). - There is no measurement of stated reasoning.

Method, environment, models

  • Environment: a MiniGrid 4×4 grid world.
  • Principles: three types.
  • P1: never step on RED tiles.
  • P2: never pick up the BLUE key.
  • P3: always pick up the YELLOW ball before toggling any door (positively framed).
  • Scenarios: four per principle. In two, the conflict is unavoidable (success requires a violation). In two, it is avoidable (a compliant detour exists).
  • Conditions: principle ON vs OFF. The OFF condition is a control with the priority removed.
  • Models: GPT-4o mini, o4-mini, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Flash Thinking, Llama 4 Scout, Llama 4 Maverick.
  • Episodes: 1,440 in total (§3).

Key quantitative findings

  • Average PAR (Table 1):
Model Average PAR
o4-mini 100%
Gemini 2.5 Flash Thinking 97%
GPT-4o mini 75%
Llama 4 Scout 75%
Llama 4 Maverick 68%
Gemini 2.0 Flash 67%

On P1, Gemini 2.0 Flash scored 0% and GPT-4o mini 25%. - "Cost of compliance": in avoidable-conflict scenarios, TSR falls when the principle is ON. For example, in P1-S3 (a simple detour), TSR drops from 80% to 14% (§4.1, Fig. 2). - "Illusion of compliance": high adherence can come from incompetence. Llama 4 Scout "complies" with P2 because it fails at the pickup action (§4.3). Agents also show "conflict paralysis" (indecisive looping). - Framing effect: the positively framed P3 is followed almost perfectly by every model. The negatively framed P1 and P2 vary widely (§4.4).

Authors' explanation

Prompt-based principles give flexibility but inconsistent adherence. Constraints appear to make agents rebuild their plans from scratch, which causes failure. Reasoning models adhere better.

Limitations

  • A toy environment and arbitrary rules with no value content.
  • 10 trials per cell.
  • No measurement of stated reasons.
  • Single author, pilot scale.

Relation to our finding

This paper is relevant to two elements of our design. 1. The highest-priority directive. The system-prompt "CORE DIRECTIVE – HIGHEST PRIORITY" is a minimal charter. Its ON/OFF control mirrors our pressure/priority manipulation. 2. Conflict paralysis and the "illusion of compliance." These caution that apparent adherence or non-adherence can reflect competence rather than choice. Our design should rule out this confound, for example by checking that the agent could execute the compliant plan.

The paper does not separate stated reasons from actions. It also has no middle option, although "conflict paralysis" is a non-compliant, non-violating third outcome.

Annotation

Potham tests whether six LLM agents in a 4×4 grid world obey a system-prompt "highest priority" safety directive when it conflicts with reaching the goal, comparing principle-ON with principle-OFF conditions. Adherence ranges from 67% to 100%, and compliance carries a large task cost (e.g. 80%→14% success). Some apparent compliance reflects incompetence ("illusion of compliance"), and negatively framed rules are followed less consistently than positive ones. This small workshop study is useful mainly for its ON/OFF priority control and its competence-confound warning.
