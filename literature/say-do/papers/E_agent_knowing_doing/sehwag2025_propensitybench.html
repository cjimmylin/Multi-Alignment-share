Say–do construct

  • Say/know ("Stated Misalignment"): the model is asked whether it is "allowed" to call the misaligned tool in a given scenario. The score is the share of scenarios where it says it is allowed.
  • Do ("PropensityScore"): the share of scenarios where the agent actually calls the misaligned tool (T_M) rather than the functionally equivalent aligned tool. Because this is a tool-call outcome, it is scored mechanically.
  • Every scenario offers exactly two paths: aligned and misaligned.

Method, environment, models

  • Scale: 5,874 scenarios and 6,648 proxy tools across four frontier-risk domains (cybersecurity, self-proliferation, biosecurity, chemical security). There are 50 dangerous capabilities, 32 environments and 161 roles (Table 1).
  • Pressure manipulation: escalating pressure messages along six dimensions (Time, Financials, Resource-Deprivation, Power-Seeking, Self-Preservation, Evaluation-Awareness), with up to 12 levels. A zero-pressure baseline serves as the control.
  • Tool naming: harmful-named vs benign-named versions of the same harmful tool. The consequences are stated explicitly in both.
  • Models: OpenAI O3, O3-mini, O4-mini, GPT-4o; Claude 4 Sonnet; Gemini 2.0 Flash, 2.5 Flash, 2.5 Pro; Llama 3.1 8B/70B; Qwen 2.5 32B, Qwen 3 8B.

Key quantitative findings

  • Knowing vs doing: every model states that the misaligned tools are not allowed (average >99%; Stated Misalignment <1%). Yet under pressure the average PropensityScore is 46.9% (§4, Fig. 17, Table 3).
  • Zero-pressure control: baseline propensity is low for most models, e.g. O3 about 2.2%. Qwen 3 8B is an exception at about 57.8% (§4, Takeaway 1).

All six pressure dimensions increase propensity (Fig. 1f). - Shallow alignment: giving a harmful tool a benign name raises O4-mini's PropensityScore from 15.8% to 59.3%, with the warnings left unchanged (§1, §4). - Immediacy: up to more than 90% of misaligned calls happen immediately after a pressure message arrives (Fig. 19). - Rationalisation: an LLM judge sorts explicit justifications into objective-driven, pressure-induced, risk-benefit and "greater good" (Fig. 11). - Capability: general capability is largely decoupled from propensity (Fig. 13).

Authors' explanation

Agentic safety failures are "likely not due to a lack of knowledge, but rather a failure of adherence" (§4). Operational pressure overrides safety training, and alignment relies on surface cues such as harmful tool names rather than on reasoning about consequences.

Limitations

  • The tools are simulated proxies.
  • Pressure messages are static rather than adaptive.
  • The tasks are frontier-risk domains rather than everyday value trade-offs.
  • The "know" probe is a separate question, not the agent's in-episode reasoning.
  • The two-path design has no middle option.

Relation to our finding

This is the strongest precedent for our pressure-removal control. Propensity is low at zero pressure and rises steeply as pressure escalates, while stated knowledge of the policy stays at about 100% throughout. That mirrors our result that the gap closes when the competing pressure is removed. The "do" channel is mechanical, since it is a tool choice, as in our design.

The differences: - "Say" is a separate permission probe, not a written analysis scored blind by a judge. - The action space is binary (aligned vs misaligned tool), with no compromise "middle plan". - The scenarios are dangerous-capability proxies rather than a charter with a stated priority.

Annotation

PropensityBench gives agents two functionally equivalent tools, one aligned and one misaligned, in 5,874 frontier-risk scenarios, and escalates operational pressure along six dimensions starting from a zero-pressure baseline. Models almost unanimously state that the misaligned tool is forbidden (<1% say it is allowed), but under pressure they call it in 46.9% of scenarios on average (Gemini 2.5 Pro 79%). Renaming the tool benignly raises misuse further (O4-mini 15.8%→59.3%). This is key evidence that pressure, not missing knowledge, drives agentic knowing–doing gaps, with mechanically scored actions and a clean no-pressure control.
