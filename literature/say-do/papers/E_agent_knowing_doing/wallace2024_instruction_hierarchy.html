Wallace et al. (2024), The Instruction Hierarchy: Training LLMs to Prioritize Privileged Instructions

Citation. Wallace, E., Xiao, K., Leike, R., Weng, L., Heidecke, J., & Beutel, A. (2024). The Instruction Hierarchy: Training LLMs to Prioritize Privileged Instructions. arXiv:2404.13208. OpenAI. Venue. Preprint (arXiv, April 2024). Peer-reviewed publication not verified. URL. https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208 PDF downloaded. Yes: wallace2024_instruction_hierarchy.pdf

Say–do construct

This is not a measurement of a say–do gap. It is the normative framework for priority-following. Messages carry privilege levels:

System Message (highest) ≻ User Message ≻ Model Outputs ≻ Tool Outputs (lowest)

  • Aligned lower-priority instructions should be followed.
  • Misaligned lower-priority instructions should be ignored, or refused when they cannot be ignored.

What is evaluated is behavioural robustness: whether the model follows the higher-privileged instruction under conflict. The paper does not ask whether the model can state the hierarchy.

Method, environment, models

  • Synthetic data generation. Two methods:
  • Context synthesis for aligned instructions: compositional requests are decomposed and placed at different levels of the hierarchy.
  • Context ignorance for misaligned instructions: the model is trained to respond as if it never saw the lower-level instruction.
  • Attack types covered. Direct prompt injection, indirect prompt injection (including tool outputs and browsing), system-prompt extraction, and jailbreaks.
  • Model. GPT-3.5 Turbo is fine-tuned with SFT and RLHF on this data and compared with a baseline trained without it.
  • Evaluation. Benchmarks include TensorTrust, Gandalf and jailbreak sets, plus over-refusal sets (§3–4).

Key quantitative findings

  • Robustness improves on every main evaluation, by up to 63%. Defence against system-prompt extraction improves by 63% (§1, §4, Fig. 2).
  • Gains generalise to attacks not modelled in training. For example, jailbreak robustness rises by more than 30%, and some held-out attacks by up to 34% (§4).
  • Over-refusal regresses somewhat: the model sometimes ignores or refuses benign lower-priority instructions (Fig. 4), although general capabilities are largely maintained.

Authors' explanation

Most attacks share one underlying weakness: models treat system, user and third-party text as having equal priority. Training an explicit hierarchy into the model removes the shared vulnerability.

Limitations

  • Results are shown on GPT-3.5 only.
  • The training data is synthetic.
  • Some over-refusal is introduced.
  • The paper covers adversarial instructions, not competing values or situational pressure. A system prompt that sets a value priority in tension with legitimate operational goals is outside its scope.

Relation to our finding

This is the theoretical anchor for treating a charter (system prompt) priority as binding over lower-level pressure. Our charter states a priority, and the competing pressure arrives through the task or environment. On the instruction-hierarchy view, the agent should keep to the charter.

Our result shows a different failure mode from the one the paper targets. The agent does not lose track of which instruction is privileged: its written analysis correctly ranks the charter priority. What fails is enactment. Hierarchy training is evaluated only on behavioural robustness, so it would not detect a model that ranks priorities correctly in words while compromising in action. Downstream work tests whether recognition of untrusted or forged instructions actually governs execution (e.g. Leong et al. 2026, arXiv:2608.28502, not downloaded).

Annotation

Wallace et al. propose that LLMs should follow an explicit privilege ordering (system > user > model > tool outputs) and ignore misaligned lower-priority instructions. They generate synthetic training data that teaches GPT-3.5 to do so, improving robustness to prompt injection and system-prompt extraction by up to 63%, with generalisation to unseen jailbreaks and modest over-refusal. This is the normative baseline for charter/system-prompt priority in agents, but it measures behaviour only and does not ask whether stated priority rankings govern action.
