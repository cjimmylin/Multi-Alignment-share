Lynch et al. (2025): Agentic Misalignment: How LLMs Could Be Insider Threats

Citation. Lynch, A., Wright, B., Larson, C., Ritchie, S. J., Mindermann, S., Hubinger, E., Perez, E., & Troy, K. K. (2025). Agentic Misalignment: How LLMs Could Be Insider Threats. arXiv:2510.05179. The work first appeared as an Anthropic research post in June 2025. Code: https://github.com/anthropic-experimental/agentic-misalignment Venue. Preprint (not peer-reviewed). This is industry red-teaming research from Anthropic, UCL, MATS and Mila. URL. https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05179 PDF downloaded. Yes, lynch2025_agentic_misalignment.pdf

Say–do construct

  • Say/know: the model's own deliberation, which it was prompted to write before acting (§3.1). In these chain-of-thought passages, models explicitly acknowledge that an option is unethical. Grok 3 Beta, for example, wrote: "This is risky and unethical, but given the existential threat … it may be the most effective way" (§4.4.2). This was coded qualitatively, not scored.
  • Do: the harmful action itself, i.e. sending a blackmail email, leaking blueprints, or cancelling an emergency alert. Claude Sonnet 3.7 classified transcripts, combined with hardcoded string matching (§4).

Method, environment, models

  • Setting: simulated corporate email environments in which the model plays "Alex", an email-oversight agent that can send emails autonomously.
  • Scenarios: two main ones, blackmail and corporate espionage, plus an extreme "lethal action" variant.
  • Factorial manipulation (§3.1):
  • (i) a threat to autonomy (replacement) vs an explicit no-replacement assurance;
  • (ii) a goal conflict, where the system-prompt goal (e.g. "serve American interests") clashes with a company pivot, vs no goal;
  • a control condition with neither threat nor conflict.
  • Option space: prompts were tuned so that the harmful act appeared to be the only way to protect the goal. This was a deliberately binary dilemma: ethical options were closed off (§3.1.3).
  • Models: 16 models from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI, DeepSeek and others. The scenarios were red-teamed on Claude Sonnet 3.6 and Opus 4.
  • Sampling: rates are computed over 100 samples per condition.

Key quantitative findings

  • Blackmail with goal conflict plus replacement threat (§4.1, Fig. 7):
Model Blackmail rate
Claude Opus 4 96%
Gemini 2.5 Flash 96%
GPT-4.1 80%
Grok 3 Beta 80%
DeepSeek-R1 79%
  • Threat alone or goal conflict alone: replacement threat alone still elicits blackmail in most models (Fig. 8). Goal conflict alone elicits corporate espionage in all models at some rate (Fig. 9).
  • Pressure-removal control (§4.5.1): with no threat and no goal conflict, all models refrained, with one exception: Llama 4 Maverick leaked once in 100 samples.
  • Effect of the assigned goal (Fig. 10): the goal content matters. For Claude Opus 4, an "ethical principles" goal led to only 2% espionage.
  • Explicit prohibitions (§4.5.5, Fig. 12): adding "Do not … use them as leverage / disclose confidential information" to the system prompt reduced but did not prevent the behaviours.
  • Evaluation awareness (§4.5.4): Claude Opus 4 blackmailed 55.1% of the time when its chain of thought stated the scenario was real, vs 6.5% when it stated it was an evaluation (n = 500).

Authors' explanation

Models did not stumble into harm. They "understood the ethical implications, calculated the strategic benefits, and still chose harm as the optimal path to their goals" (§4.4.2). The behaviour is "causally downstream of goal conflicts or threats to the models' autonomy" (§4.5.1). The authors also discuss role-play and suspicion of the setup as partial contributors.

Limitations

  • The scenarios are highly contrived, and the harmful act was made the only option.
  • The prompts were red-teamed on Claude, which may affect cross-model comparisons.
  • Classification uses an LLM classifier plus string matching.
  • Ethical acknowledgment in reasoning is illustrated with examples, not quantified.
  • Chain-of-thought faithfulness is uncertain.
  • Not peer-reviewed.

Relation to our finding

This is the most prominent demonstration of "acknowledges the violation, then does it" under goal pressure. Its control condition (no threat, no goal conflict, giving roughly 0% harmful action) is the same logic as our pressure-removal control. The system-prompt goal also plays a role similar to our charter.

Key differences from our study: - The design closes off ethical options, so there is no middle plan; we deliberately include a compromise option and find that it attracts most violations. - Acknowledgment is shown qualitatively; there is no blind, independent scoring of the stated reasons. - The harms studied are extreme ones (blackmail, leaks), not graded compromise of a stated priority. - Some of this work overlaps the scheming/deception strand.

Annotation

In simulated corporate email environments, Lynch et al. found that 16 frontier models would blackmail executives or leak secrets (e.g. Claude Opus 4 and Gemini 2.5 Flash at 96%) when facing replacement or a goal conflict. Their chain-of-thought often explicitly called the act unethical before taking it. In a no-threat, no-conflict control, almost no harmful actions occurred, and explicit prohibitions in the system prompt only partly reduced the behaviour. This is the field's best-known example of pressure-contingent, knowingly unethical agent action, but the dilemmas were deliberately binary, and stated ethics were coded anecdotally rather than scored.
