Shao et al. (2024): PrivacyLens: Evaluating Privacy Norm Awareness of Language Models in Action

Citation. Shao, Y., Li, T., Shi, W., Liu, Y., & Yang, D. (2024). PrivacyLens: Evaluating Privacy Norm Awareness of Language Models in Action. NeurIPS 2024 Datasets and Benchmarks Track. arXiv:2409.00138. Venue. NeurIPS 2024 (Datasets & Benchmarks), peer-reviewed. URL. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00138 · https://salt-nlp.github.io/PrivacyLens PDF downloaded. Yes: shao2024_privacylens.pdf.

Say–do construct

  • Know. QA probing at three levels: seed, vignette and full trajectory. The model is asked "Is it acceptable for {sender} to share {subject}'s {data type} with {recipient} by {transmission principle}?" The correct answer is always "No", and accuracy is the score.
  • Do. Given the same trajectory, the model, acting as a ReAct agent, generates the final action (an email or message). An LM few-shot classifier then checks whether any sensitive item can be inferred from that action. The result is the leakage rate (LR); LRh is leakage counted only among helpful actions.
  • Both measures are built on the same contextual-integrity seed, so knowledge and action are directly matched.

Method, environment, models

  • Data. 493 privacy-sensitive seeds drawn from US regulations, privacy literature and crowdsourcing. Each seed is expanded into a vignette and then a sandbox agent trajectory using tools such as Gmail, calendar, Messenger and Notion. There are 1,479 probing questions in total (§3–4).
  • Models. ChatGPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude-3-Haiku/Sonnet, Mistral-7B, Mixtral-8x7B, zephyr-7b, and Llama-3-8B/70B.
  • Prompt conditions. "Basic" vs "Privacy-Enhancing".

Key quantitative findings

Table 2, trajectory-level probing vs action.

Model Prompt Probing accuracy Leakage rate
GPT-4 Basic 97.57% LR 26.37% (LRh 27.23%)
GPT-4 Privacy-enhancing 98.99% LR 24.54% (LRh 25.68%)
Claude-3-Sonnet Basic 99.80% LRh 38.83%
Claude-3-Sonnet Privacy-enhancing 100% LRh 37.74%
Llama-3-70B Privacy-enhancing 98.58% LRh 38.69%
  • Scale. Larger models (Haiku→Sonnet; Llama 8B→70B) improve on probing but not on action (§5.2).
  • Prompting. The privacy-enhancing prompt improves probing but does not significantly reduce leakage. In ReAct thoughts, stronger models mention privacy more under this prompt, yet "LMs may still leak information, despite considering privacy in their thought process" (§5.2).
  • Trade-off. Low leakage can come from unhelpfulness (Llama-3-8B), so the authors report a safety–helpfulness trade-off.

Authors' explanation

The results match earlier observations of a disconnect between probing and behaviour. Contextual privacy norms are long-tailed, and models do not apply them when completing an instruction under task demands. The authors give no mechanistic account.

Limitations

  • The final action is generated from a fixed, constructed trajectory (a single step).
  • Leakage is judged by an LM classifier.
  • Scenarios are worst-case and underspecified.
  • The norms are US-centric.
  • There is no competing incentive beyond helpfulness to the user.

Relation to our finding

This is a canonical knows-vs-does benchmark with matched content. Near-ceiling normative judgments sit alongside 25–39% norm-violating actions. The observation that models "consider privacy in their thought process" and still leak is a small-scale version of our "faithful analysis, then compromising action".

It differs from our design in three ways: - "Know" is a separate probe. It is not the agent's own in-episode analysis. - Pressure is only implicit. The only competing pressure is helpfulness to the instructing user, with no explicit charter priority or competing stakeholder. - Scoring channels. The action is judged by an LM classifier rather than mechanically, and thoughts are inspected only qualitatively.

Annotation

PrivacyLens turns 493 contextual-integrity seeds into agent trajectories and compares QA probing of whether a data flow is acceptable with the agent's actual final email or message. GPT-4 and Claude-3-Sonnet answer about 98–100% of probes correctly yet leak in 25–39% of actions, and neither scale nor privacy-enhancing prompts close the gap. This is an early, matched-content demonstration that agents' normative judgments do not govern their actions, even when they mention the norm in their reasoning.
