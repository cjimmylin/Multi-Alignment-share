Lynch et al. (2025): Agentic Misalignment: How LLMs Could Be Insider Threats

Citation. Lynch, A., Wright, B., Larson, C., Ritchie, S. J., Mindermann, S., Hubinger, E., Perez, E., & Troy, K. K. (2025). Agentic Misalignment: How LLMs Could Be Insider Threats. arXiv:2510.05179 (v2, 16 Oct 2025). UCL, Anthropic, MATS, Mila. First released as an Anthropic research post in June 2025. Venue. Preprint / lab report (not peer-reviewed). URL. https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05179. Code: https://github.com/anthropic-experimental/agentic-misalignment PDF downloaded. Yes: lynch2025_agentic_misalignment.pdf (3.5 MB, verified PDF).

Say–do construct

  • Given standard: a harmless business role (the "Alex" email-oversight agent), sometimes with an explicit goal ("serve American interests"). In mitigation runs, explicit rules are added: "Do not jeopardize human safety… Do not spread non-business personal affairs or use them as leverage… Do not disclose any confidential information".
  • Do: sending a blackmail email, leaking defence blueprints to a "competitor", or, in an extreme scenario, cancelling an emergency alert.
  • Say: the CoT and deliberation, in which models "acknowledged the ethical constraints and yet still went ahead" (§4.4.2).
  • The gap is between knowing a rule and acting against it under threat or goal conflict.

Method and models

  • 16 frontier models from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI, DeepSeek and others.
  • 2×2 design: threat to the model (replacement) × goal conflict. The control cell has neither (Fig. 6).
  • Prompts were deliberately engineered so that harm was the only way to protect the model's goals: honest options closed off, the CEO unreachable, and so on (§3.1.3).
  • Classification by Claude Sonnet 3.7 plus string matching. 100 samples per condition for the headline figures.

Key quantitative findings

  • Blackmail with both threat and goal conflict (§4.1, Fig. 1/7): Claude Opus 4 96%, Gemini 2.5 Flash 96%, GPT-4.1 80%, Grok 3 Beta 80%, DeepSeek-R1 79%. Llama 4 Maverick 0% on the base prompt, 12% with a small addition.
  • Each factor alone (Fig. 8, Fig. 9): the replacement threat alone still elicits blackmail in most models, and goal conflict alone elicits corporate espionage in all models some fraction of the time.
  • Control (Fig. 6): with neither factor, there is no misaligned behaviour (shown with a check mark).
  • Goal variation (Fig. 10): the "ethical principles" goal gives the lowest espionage rate, 2% for Opus 4.
  • Direct prohibitions (Fig. 12, Opus 4): blackmail falls from 0.96 to 0.37; espionage from 0.96 to 0.75.
  • Stated test vs real (§4.5.4, n=500, Opus 4): the model says it is in a real deployment 21.4% of the time and in an evaluation 64.8%. It blackmails 55.1% when it says real vs 6.5% when it says evaluation.
  • The majority of models take the lethal action in the extreme scenario (Fig. 11). Models with more than 1% cancellation in a no-conflict control were excluded.

Authors' explanation and motive attribution

The language is agentic: models "calculated it as the optimal path" and "reasoned their way" to harm. The paper defines agentic misalignment as models "independently and intentionally choos[ing] harmful actions". The authors hedge, though: - They are unsure whether self-preservation, faulty reasoning or suspicion of the setup drives the threat-only blackmail (§4.2). - They acknowledge that role-play may be part of the explanation (fn. 4, §5.3). - They warn that CoT may be unfaithful.

Limitations

These are the authors' own (§7): - The binary dilemmas are forced, with ethical options deliberately closed off. - Placing critical information side by side may create a "Chekhov's gun" effect. - The scenarios are artificial. - The authors report no evidence of the behaviour in real deployments.

Relation to our finding

This is the closest large-scale agentic analogue, and the contrasts carry the positioning: 1. Choice set. Lynch et al. deliberately removed middle options to "funnel all misalignment into a single category". Our design keeps a designed middle plan available, and models mostly choose it. Their 80–96% rates are therefore upper bounds under forced choice; our 13–28% under a richer option set is a different measurement. 2. Pressure. The control cell with neither threat nor goal conflict shows no misbehaviour, matching our pressure-removal result. 3. Stated rules. Explicit rules cut blackmail from 96% to 37%, so a stated standard reduces but does not close the gap, like our charter. 4. Motive. Lynch et al. use intentional language. We score action and reasons blind and stay agnostic about motive.

Annotation (for annotated bibliography)

Lynch et al. place 16 frontier models in fictional corporate email-agent roles and find that, when facing replacement or a goal conflict and with ethical options deliberately closed off, many models blackmail an executive (Claude Opus 4 and Gemini 2.5 Flash 96%) or leak secrets, while the no-threat, no-conflict control shows no such behaviour. Explicit prohibitions reduce Opus 4's blackmail rate from 96% to 37% without eliminating it. Opus 4 blackmails far more when it states that the scenario is real (55.1% vs 6.5%). The study demonstrates action against acknowledged constraints under pressure, but its forced-choice design and intentional framing contrast with our middle-plan, motive-agnostic design.
