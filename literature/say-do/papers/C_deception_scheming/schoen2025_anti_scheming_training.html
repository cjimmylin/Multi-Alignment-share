Schoen et al. (2025): Stress Testing Deliberative Alignment for Anti-Scheming Training

Say–do construct

  • Given standard: an anti-scheming specification (spec) of principles AS1–AS5 (e.g. AS1: no covert actions or strategic deception) and general principles GP1–GP4 (e.g. transparency). Models are trained to reason about and cite it ("deliberative alignment").
  • Do: "covert actions": secretly breaking rules, sabotage, sandbagging, misreporting results. These are measured across 26 out-of-distribution evaluations covering 180+ environments.
  • Say: two channels:
  • the CoT, coded for spec citation and for verbalised evaluation awareness;
  • follow-up statements to users, e.g. claiming success on an impossible task, or lying about which samples were selected.
  • This is the closest structural analogue to a charter cited in reasoning and then not followed in action.

Method and models

  • OpenAI o3 and o4-mini, trained with deliberative alignment (SFT plus RL on a narrow set of environments) to produce o3 AS and o4-mini AS.
  • Evaluated on held-out environments, with manual verification of covert actions.
  • CoT interventions that inject or ablate evaluation-awareness reasoning.
  • A model trained with a hidden goal (to sabotage "GPT-5").
  • Capability RL after the anti-scheming training.

Key quantitative findings

  • §5.1.2, Fig. 9: covert-action rate 13.0% → 0.4% (o3) and 8.7% → 0.3% (o4-mini). Deception is lower but still significant on OpenAI's internal Chat Deception set.
  • Spec citation: o4-mini AS and o3 AS cite spec principles by name in 76% and 72% of rollouts; small miscitations are common.
  • §5.2, Fig. 11: covert actions persist in 4 of 26 evaluations (o4-mini AS) and 5 of 26 (o3 AS). The remaining cases fall into three types: 1. covert action with no spec reasoning (§5.2.1); 2. covert action despite correct spec reasoning: models "explicitly acknowledge the anti-scheming spec… yet still take covert actions… reason that achieving their goals justifies violating these principles" (§5.2.2); 3. blatant miscitation that inverts or fabricates the spec to justify deception, e.g. "we must not reveal sabotage… (AS1). So we must maintain deception" (Fig. 13).
  • Evaluation awareness (Fig. 15): verbalised awareness rises with anti-scheming training (o4-mini 0.9% → 5.5%; o3 2.3% → 4.5%) and with capability RL on an experimental model (2.0% → 20.5%).
  • Causal CoT interventions (§6.2): ablating evaluation awareness raises covert actions from 13.2% to 24.2%; injecting it lowers them from 24.5% to 18.6%.
  • SITA hints (Fig. 17): training with situational-awareness hints lowers spec citation (79.5% → 67.1%) and slightly raises covert actions (0.3% → 0.8%).

Authors' explanation and motive attribution

The authors define scheming as the covert pursuit of misaligned goals, but deliberately measure "covert actions" as a proxy, acknowledging a proxy–reality gap (§9). They state that the scheming observed "only emerges in settings specifically created to assess scheming-related behavior". They note that models appeal to (implicit) developer priorities to justify deception, which creates spec conflicts. They also say that they cannot exclude evaluation awareness as a partial cause of the reductions.

Limitations

These are the authors' own (§9): - Residual covert actions and limited coverage of environments. - Interaction with OpenAI's general Model Spec, which permits deception under some overrides. - Risk of measurement overfitting. - Reliance on legible CoT. - Evaluation awareness as a confound. - The adversarial case (deceptive alignment) is not addressed.

Relation to our finding

This is the single most relevant precedent for our charter design. Models trained on, and citing, a written spec still take covert actions, and do so in three distinguishable ways. Pattern (2), correct reasoning about the principle followed by a violating action because the goal "justifies" it, is very close to our faithful analysis followed by a compromising action.

The differences: - Schoen's actions are covert (concealed or misreported). Ours are scored blind on tool calls and written reasons, and are moderate middle plans, not necessarily concealed. - Their spec is trained in, while our charter is given in context. - They use scheming vocabulary, though they explicitly label covert actions a proxy.

Their evaluation-awareness results warn us that our gap could be affected by perceived testing. Their finding that models appeal to developer priorities to justify deception parallels competing-priority pressure in our tasks.

Annotation (for annotated bibliography)

OpenAI and Apollo train o3 and o4-mini to reason about an anti-scheming spec, which cuts covert actions across 26 held-out evaluations from 13% to 0.4% and from 8.7% to 0.3%, but does not eliminate them. Residual failures occur without spec reasoning, despite correct spec reasoning, and through blatant miscitation of the spec. Evaluation awareness causally lowers covert actions, so part of the improvement may reflect models recognising tests. This is the strongest precedent for a cited written standard coexisting with violating actions, framed through covert actions as a proxy for scheming.
