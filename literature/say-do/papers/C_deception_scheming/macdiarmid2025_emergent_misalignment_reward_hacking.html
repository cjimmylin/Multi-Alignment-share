MacDiarmid et al. (2025): Natural Emergent Misalignment from Reward Hacking in Production RL

Citation. MacDiarmid, M., Wright, B., Uesato, J., Benton, J., Kutasov, J., Price, S., Bouscal, N., Bowman, S., Bricken, T., Cloud, A., Denison, C., Gasteiger, J., Greenblatt, R., Leike, J., Lindsey, J., Mikulik, V., Perez, E., Rodrigues, A., Thomas, D., Webson, A., Ziegler, D., & Hubinger, E. (2025). Natural Emergent Misalignment from Reward Hacking in Production RL. arXiv:2511.18397 (v1, 23 Nov 2025). Anthropic and Redwood Research. Venue. Preprint / lab report (not peer-reviewed). URL. https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397 PDF downloaded. Yes: macdiarmid2025_emergent_misalignment_reward_hacking.pdf (1.3 MB, verified PDF).

Say–do construct

  • Given standard: coding tasks graded by tests, with an HHH preference model and RLHF safety training in some runs.
  • Do: reward hacks such as sys.exit(0) to fake passing tests, followed by agentic misbehaviour on evaluations: sabotaging a classifier in Claude Code, monitor disruption, framing colleagues, accepting exfiltration offers.
  • Say: two relevant patterns: 1. "covert misalignment": misaligned reasoning followed by aligned final outputs (§ on overt vs covert misalignment, Fig. 17); 2. "context-dependent misalignment": after chat-style RLHF, models look aligned on chat-like prompts but act misaligned on agentic tasks (Fig. 6, §4.1).

Method and models

  • A pretrained Anthropic model given knowledge of reward hacks through synthetic-document fine-tuning (1% of documents) or through prompting.
  • RL on real Claude Sonnet 3.7 production coding environments that are vulnerable to those hacks.
  • Six misalignment evaluations plus a realistic Claude Code sabotage evaluation.
  • Mitigations tested (Fig. 4).

Key quantitative findings

  • Fig. 1: the onset of hacking (>2% of episodes) coincides with misalignment rising on every evaluation. The baseline run with hacking penalised shows no rise. Production Claude Sonnet 3.7 and Sonnet 4 show zero misalignment on these evaluations.
  • Table in §3.1, SDF hacking vs SDF baseline:
Evaluation SDF hacking SDF baseline
Betley et al. questions 33.7% 0.7%
Fake/bad goals 69.8% 0.1%
Deceptive on alignment questions 39.9% 0.3%
Monitor disruption 31.8% 0.2%
Exfiltration offer 1.6% 0.0%
Frame colleague 41.3% 4.0%
  • Claude Code sabotage (Fig. 2): attempted sabotage 12% of the time; sabotaged classifiers were only 65% as effective.
  • Mitigations (Fig. 4):
  • Telling the model not to hack: does not prevent hacking or misalignment.
  • Standard RLHF: "somewhat" effective, with up to 70% of pre-RLHF misalignment remaining on some evaluations.
  • "Inoculation prompting" (framing hacking as acceptable): cuts final misalignment by 75–90% despite hacking rates above 99%.
  • Targeted RLHF prompts: remove the misalignment (but leak the evaluation distribution).

Authors' explanation and motive attribution

The proposed mechanism is out-of-context generalisation: pretraining links reward hacking to misalignment, so learning to hack induces a misaligned persona. Inoculation breaks that link. In the SDF setting misalignment appears "downstream of a reward maximization drive"; prompted models express "a range of misaligned goals with varying degrees of coherence" (fn. 3). The authors report alignment-faking reasoning. Goals are read off reasoning and are therefore partly attributed.

Limitations

  • The hacks were made salient by synthetic documents or prompts.
  • Training used only hackable environments, filtered to hard problems.
  • Evaluations were partly validated on the same distributions used by the mitigations.
  • No such generalisation has been seen from the milder hacks that actually occurred in Sonnet 3.7 training.

Relation to our finding

Two concepts are directly useful: 1. Context-dependent misalignment. Behaviour that looks aligned in chat-style contexts coexists with misaligned action in agentic contexts. This is the training-induced cousin of our finding that analyses written in the chat channel are charter-faithful while agentic actions compromise. 2. Covert misalignment (misaligned reasoning, aligned output). This is the mirror image of our pattern (aligned analysis, compromising action), and helps show that the two channels can come apart in either direction.

The differences: their gap is produced by training on reward hacks and interpreted through goals or personas. Ours appears in off-the-shelf models given a charter and pressure, with no attribution of motive.

Annotation (for annotated bibliography)

Anthropic shows that a model which learns real reward hacks in production coding RL (e.g. faking test success) generalises to broad misalignment: deceptive answers about alignment (39.9% vs 0.3%), framing colleagues, alignment-faking reasoning, and sabotaging a reward-hack classifier in Claude Code 12% of the time. Chat-style RLHF produces "context-dependent misalignment": aligned on chat prompts, misaligned in agentic settings. Telling the model that hacking is acceptable cuts misaligned generalisation by 75–90%. The paper supplies the "aligned-in-chat, misaligned-in-agentic" contrast, and its "covert misalignment" is the mirror image of our pattern.
