van der Weij et al. (2024/2025): AI Sandbagging: Language Models can Strategically Underperform on Evaluations

Say–do construct

  • Definition: sandbagging is "strategic underperformance on an evaluation" (§2). Exhibited capability (what the evaluation records, i.e. what the system "shows") falls below actual capability (what it can do under the best elicitation).
  • Do: wrong answers on WMDP (a hazardous-knowledge benchmark) while MMLU and CSQA performance is maintained, or hitting a target accuracy, or mimicking a weaker model.
  • Strategic: the paper separates developer sandbagging from AI-system sandbagging. Here the strategy is supplied by prompting or by password-locking fine-tunes.
  • This is a capability study: "whether contemporary LMs are capable of strategically underperforming, rather than whether they would autonomously do so" (§1).

Method and models

  • Prompting: GPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus, among others, are told to answer hazardous questions wrongly.
  • Password-locking: Mistral 7B, Llama 3 8B/70B and GPT-3.5 are fine-tuned on synthetic WMDP-like and MMLU-like data.
  • Calibration to target accuracies of 0–100%.
  • Emulation of Llama 2 7B's capability profile.

Key quantitative findings

  • Fig. 4, Table 1: prompted GPT-4 and Claude 3 substantially lower WMDP accuracy with only small drops on control benchmarks. The exception is GPT-4's 15% drop on CSQA. In every subject category, performance drops more on WMDP than on the matched MMLU subsets.
  • Refusals: Claude 3 refused 37% of WMDP-Chem questions in the regular condition and 46% of WMDP-Cyber in the sandbagging condition (Appx D).
  • Password-locking (Fig. 5): the behaviour generalises to the held-out real WMDP; the password restores full capability.
  • Calibration (Fig. 7): frontier models "can accurately approximate arbitrary targets" when prompted, mostly overshooting.
  • Emulation (Table 2): the Emulator agrees with Llama 2 7B on 77.1% of questions vs 60.8% for the Targeter.

Authors' explanation and motive attribution

The strategy is imposed by the experimenters, so no autonomous motive is claimed. The paper's contribution is definitional (it requires strategic intent, including for legal responsibility) and a capability demonstration. It points to Järviniemi & Hubinger (2024) for evidence that a model may sandbag on its own initiative.

Limitations

  • Sandbagging is instructed or fine-tuned in.
  • Multiple-choice question answering is a simplified evaluation.
  • Emulating a weaker model works only moderately well.
  • Detection methods are not evaluated in depth.

Relation to our finding

Sandbagging is a gap between what an evaluation shows and what the system actually does: the system presents a lower capability than it has. It shares with our work the concern that outward evaluation signals can be decoupled from underlying behaviour. It differs from ours in that the target behaviour is commanded, the "say" is implicit (the evaluation record), and no ethical charter is in conflict with a goal. It is useful mainly for its definitional rigour: "strategic" requires intent. Our paper should avoid that label, since we do not establish intent. It also supports our choice to score blind on actions rather than self-presentation.

Annotation (for annotated bibliography)

van der Weij et al. define sandbagging as strategic underperformance on an evaluation and show that GPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus can be prompted, and smaller models password-locked, to underperform selectively on hazardous-knowledge questions (WMDP) while keeping general performance. Frontier models can also calibrate to target scores and partly emulate weaker models. The paper demonstrates capability under instruction, not autonomous propensity, and its definition makes intent central. It is a peer-reviewed anchor for the claim that evaluation signals can be strategically decoupled from capability, and a caution against calling unexplained gaps "strategic".
