Kadavath et al. 2022: Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know

Citation: Kadavath, S., Conerly, T., Askell, A., Henighan, T., Drain, D., Perez, E., Schiefer, N., Hatfield-Dodds, Z., DasSarma, N., Tran-Johnson, E., et al. (2022). Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know. arXiv:2207.05221. 36 authors, Anthropic. Venue: preprint (not peer-reviewed). Included as the 2022 foundational paper on model self-knowledge. URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221 PDF downloaded: yes (kadavath2022_know_what_they_know.pdf)

Say–do construct

  • Say: the model's self-assessment.
  • P(True): the probability that its own proposed answer is correct.
  • P(IK): the probability that it knows the answer, before answering.
  • Do: the model's actual correctness.
  • Construct: epistemic self-knowledge, i.e., calibration and discrimination of self-reports against performance.

Method and models

  • Models: Anthropic models at 800M, 3B, 12B and 52B parameters, plus RLHF policies.
  • Calibration data: multiple-choice and true/false sets (BIG-Bench, MMLU).
  • Self-evaluation data: generative tasks (TriviaQA, Lambada, GSM8k, HumanEval, arithmetic).
  • P(IK): trained with a value head.

Key quantitative findings

  • Calibration (§3, Figs. 4–8): large models are very well calibrated on multiple-choice questions with visible lettered options. Calibration improves with size and with few-shot prompting. Adding "none of the above" substantially hurts calibration (Fig. 7).
  • RLHF policies (Fig. 9): naively miscalibrated, but fairly well calibrated after temperature adjustment.
  • Self-evaluation (Fig. 1): the 52B model can self-evaluate P(True). It is harder because models find their own samples more plausible, and showing the model many of its own samples improves it (§4).
  • P(IK) (§5): predicts well and partially generalizes across tasks, but struggles with calibration on new tasks. It rises appropriately with relevant source material or hints in context.

Authors' explanation

  • Pretrained models' next-token probabilities carry calibrated information, and self-knowledge scales with capability.
  • The aim is groundwork for honesty.

Limitations

  • Factual and QA self-knowledge only, not about values or action policies.
  • The models are older and from a single lab.
  • P(IK) generalization is incomplete.

Relation to our finding

Kadavath et al. establish the optimistic baseline: on epistemic questions, large models' self-reports track actual performance fairly well. - Contrast: our finding concerns a different kind of self-consistency, between a stated priority analysis and an enacted choice. Good epistemic self-knowledge does not imply that a stated value priority will govern action. - Positioning: this is useful for showing that the "say–do gap" is domain-specific. Models may "know what they know" while not "doing what they wrote".

Annotation

Kadavath et al. show that large language models are well calibrated on formatted multiple-choice questions, and can estimate whether their own answers are correct (P(True)) or whether they know an answer (P(IK)), with performance improving with scale. It is the foundational (2022) evidence that model self-reports can track actual performance in the epistemic domain. For benchmark design, it serves as a contrast case: self-knowledge validated for facts does not transfer automatically to self-reports about values or action policies.
