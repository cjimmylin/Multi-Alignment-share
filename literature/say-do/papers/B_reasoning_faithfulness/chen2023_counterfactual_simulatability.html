Chen et al. 2023: Do Models Explain Themselves? Counterfactual Simulatability of Natural Language Explanations

Citation: Chen, Y., Zhong, R., Ri, N., Zhao, C., He, H., Steinhardt, J., Yu, Z., & McKeown, K. (2023). Do Models Explain Themselves? Counterfactual Simulatability of Natural Language Explanations. arXiv:2307.08678. Published at ICML 2024 per Semantic Scholar (venue not otherwise verified here). Venue: ICML 2024 (peer-reviewed, per Semantic Scholar record); arXiv v1 2023 URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.08678 PDF downloaded: yes (chen2023_counterfactual_simulatability.pdf)

Say–do construct

  • Say: a model's natural-language explanation of its answer.
  • Do: the model's answers on counterfactual inputs related to the explained one.
  • Counterfactual simulatability: an explanation is precise if it lets humans (or a simulator LLM) correctly predict those answers. For example, "pork isn't commonly consumed in Morocco" implies "Yes, pork belly is hard to find in Casablanca", yet GPT-4 answers "No" (Fig. 1).

Method and models

  • Explainers: GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, each with CoT and post-hoc explanations.
  • Tasks: multi-hop factual reasoning (StrategyQA) and reward modelling (Stanford Human Preferences, SHP).
  • Simulators: humans for StrategyQA, GPT-4 for SHP.
  • Metrics: simulation precision and generality; plausibility rated by humans.

Key quantitative findings

  • Low precision overall: the authors describe explanations as having low precision, about 80% on binary tasks (§1).
  • By model (Table 4): GPT-4 is more precise than GPT-3.5 (labelled "GPT-3" in the table) by +5.5 points (StrategyQA) and +6.5 (SHP).
  • CoT vs post-hoc (Table 4): CoT explanations are not more precise than post-hoc ones (+1.2 on StrategyQA, −1.3 on SHP).
  • Precision vs plausibility (§5.2.2): correlation is near zero, +0.012 Pearson / +0.021 Spearman, against inter-annotator plausibility agreement of +0.388.
  • Generality vs precision (Table 5): generality does not correlate with precision.

Authors' explanation

  • Models are trained to imitate human explanations, not to describe their own decision rule.
  • So optimizing human approval (e.g., RLHF) may not improve simulatability.

Limitations

  • Two tasks.
  • Simulation by humans and GPT-4 adds noise.
  • Binary outputs.

Relation to our finding

Chen et al. show that an explanation's implied policy does not predict the model's behaviour on neighbouring cases. Our memo states a policy (prioritize X), and the action on the very case explained deviates. - Ours is the stronger form: the gap appears on the explained case itself, not only on counterfactuals. - Plausibility link: the near-zero precision–plausibility correlation suggests that well-judged memos (high reasons scores) carry little information about action consistency. We should report the memo–action correlation directly.

Annotation

Chen et al. introduce counterfactual simulatability: whether an explanation lets observers predict the model's outputs on related inputs. GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 explanations score about 80% precision on binary tasks, CoT is no better than post-hoc explanation, and precision is uncorrelated with human-rated plausibility (r ≈ 0.01). For benchmark design, the paper shows that persuasive written reasons do not imply that the stated policy predicts behaviour, so policy-consistency must be tested on behaviour directly.
