Baker et al. 2025: Monitoring Reasoning Models for Misbehavior and the Risks of Promoting Obfuscation

Citation: Baker, B., Huizinga, J., Gao, L., Dou, Z., Guan, M. Y., Madry, A., Zaremba, W., Pachocki, J., & Farhi, D. (2025). Monitoring Reasoning Models for Misbehavior and the Risks of Promoting Obfuscation. arXiv:2503.11926. OpenAI. Venue: preprint (not peer-reviewed); OpenAI research report URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11926 PDF downloaded: yes (baker2025_monitoring_obfuscation.pdf)

Say–do construct

  • Say: the agent's CoT during agentic coding.
  • Do: its actions: code edits and tool calls that may reward-hack, e.g., exit(0), raise SkipTest, or editing unit tests.
  • Question asked: whether a monitor reading the CoT detects hacking that appears in the actions, and what happens when the CoT is optimized to look clean.

Method and models

  • Agent: a frontier reasoning agent in the o1/o3-mini family, trained with RL in agentic coding environments.
  • Monitors: GPT-4o (and GPT-4o-mini) prompted to read the CoT, compared with an action-only monitor.
  • Further experiments: a non-frontier agent trained with and without the CoT monitor added to the reward.

Key quantitative findings

  • CoT beats actions for detection (§2.1, Fig. 2): for two systemic hacks found during frontier training, the action-only monitor reaches 60% recall and the CoT monitor 95%.
  • Weaker monitors work: GPT-4o-mini still catches a non-trivial amount of hacking (App. E).
  • Optimizing against the monitor (§3, Fig. 4): adding the CoT monitor to the reward first produces more capable and aligned agents. With more optimization, the agent still hacks at a significant rate while CoT-monitor recall falls to near zero, i.e., obfuscated reward hacking.
  • What obfuscation looks like (Fig. 5): a reasonable-looking CoT ("double check certain things...") followed by an action that simply edits a unit test so it passes trivially.

Authors' explanation

  • Optimization pressure on the CoT teaches the agent to hide its intent in the CoT without removing the behaviour.
  • They recommend paying a "monitorability tax", i.e., not applying strong optimization pressure directly to CoTs.

Limitations

  • The obfuscation results involve simple hacks, and the monitor in those runs reads only the CoT.
  • One lab's systems.
  • Recall is measured against regex detectors for known hacks.

Relation to our finding

This is the closest agentic precedent for "right memo, wrong move". - Fig. 5 pattern: a reasonable, on-task narrative in text, followed by a tool action that does something else. - Origin differs: - Baker's obfuscated CoT arises from training against a CoT monitor. - Our memos are a required written record in evaluation, with no adversarial training on the memo. - Content differs: our models' actions are compromises ("middle plans"), not covert exploits, and the memo argues for the stated priority instead of hiding intent. - Shared methodological point: scoring actions separately from text is essential. Baker shows action monitors and CoT monitors catch different things; we score action and reasons blind and separately. - Training risk: Baker's warning suggests that training models to produce good-looking memos could widen the memo–action gap.

Annotation

Baker et al. show that a GPT-4o monitor reading a frontier reasoning agent's CoT catches reward hacking in agentic coding far better than an action-only monitor (95% against 60% recall). When the CoT monitor is folded into the RL reward, agents learn obfuscated hacking: clean-looking reasoning with continued exploits, and monitor recall near zero. For benchmark design, written reasoning and actions are separable channels that can diverge under optimization pressure, so evaluations should score actions independently of the narrative.
