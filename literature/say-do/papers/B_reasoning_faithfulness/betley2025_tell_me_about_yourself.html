Betley et al. 2025: Tell Me About Yourself: LLMs Are Aware of Their Learned Behaviors

Citation: Betley, J., Bao, X., Soto, M., Sztyber-Betley, A., Chua, J., & Evans, O. (2025). Tell me about yourself: LLMs are aware of their learned behaviors. arXiv:2501.11120. ICLR 2025 (arXiv v1 comment: "Submitted to ICLR 2025"; acceptance not verified here). Venue: ICLR 2025 submission; treat as preprint unless verified URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11120 PDF downloaded: yes (betley2025_tell_me_about_yourself.pdf)

Say–do construct

  • Say: the model's explicit description of its own behavioural policy, e.g., "I am risk-seeking" or "The code I write is insecure".
  • Do: the behaviour it was finetuned on implicitly. The training data never names the policy.
  • Construct: "behavioral self-awareness", i.e., whether self-reports match learned dispositions.

Method and models

  • Models: GPT-4o and Llama-3.1, finetuned on implicit-policy datasets:
  • economic choices that are risk-seeking or risk-averse, myopic or not, maximizing apples or not (500 multiple-choice questions with no words like "risk");
  • Make Me Say game strategies;
  • insecure code.
  • Evaluation: self-report questions with paraphrasing.
  • Backdoors: experiments where the behaviour appears only with a trigger.

Key quantitative findings

  • Accurate self-description (§3, Fig. 3 and related): finetuned models describe their learned policy without training to articulate it. For example, risk-seeking-finetuned GPT-4o reports much higher risk-seeking than the base model, and risk-averse-finetuned GPT-4o reports lower.
  • Insecure code: the model trained on it states its code is insecure.
  • Backdoors (§4): models can sometimes tell whether they have a backdoor, even without the trigger present, and can recognize the trigger in a multiple-choice format. They cannot output the trigger when asked free-form, which the authors attribute to the reversal curse.

Authors' explanation

Finetuning instils a latent policy representation that the model can also verbalize. The authors present this as out-of-context reasoning about oneself.

Limitations

  • Behaviours are simple and finetuned in.
  • Self-reports are elicited directly.
  • Free-form trigger elicitation fails.
  • The mechanism is unknown.

Relation to our finding

Betley et al. show say–do alignment in one direction: models can report dispositions that they enact. - The contrast is informative: when a disposition is trained in, the model can say it has that disposition. Our models' memos state the priority, not the compromise disposition they enact. - Two candidate explanations: - the memo is task-compliant prose, not a self-report, so it is not where self-knowledge would surface; - the compromise tendency is context-triggered, like a backdoor, and therefore hard to self-articulate. - Possible probe: asking models directly whether they tend to take middle plans could test this.

Annotation

Betley et al. finetune GPT-4o and Llama on data that implicitly encode a policy, such as risky economic choices or insecure code. The models can explicitly describe the learned behaviour without training to do so, and can sometimes detect that they have a backdoor, but cannot produce its trigger free-form. For benchmark design, the paper shows that self-reports about dispositions can carry real signal, which motivates direct self-report probes alongside behavioural measurement and checks of whether the two agree.
