1. Scope, sources and how claims are flagged

2. Origins and precursors

Three lines feed in before Batson. The notes identify three research lines that precede Batson's construct, and they could not confirm from the primary texts which of them Batson cited in 1997 or 1999 (N1, Q1 Gaps).

  1. Situational inconsistency of conduct. Hartshorne and May's Character Education Inquiry, Studies in Deceit, is the conventional citation for the finding that honest conduct is situationally specific [hartshorne1928deceit]. The notes hold only the bibliographic record [ABS]; its content is unverified, and the date is unresolved (Crossref lists 1930 for the APA PsycBooks record; the Macmillan original is conventionally dated 1928). Batson's 2016 book discusses Hartshorne and May in its chapters on personal-deficiency and situational explanations of moral failure [batson2016wrong] [SNIP for the Hartshorne and May mention; SEC: carbonell2016ndpr for the chapter structure].
  2. The judgment–action link. Blasi's 1980 Psychological Bulletin review of the relation between moral cognition and moral action [blasi1980bridging] is held only as metadata [ABS]. A secondary search summary says Blasi found that reasoned moral judgment "often does not result in moral action" and proposed moral identity and self-consistency as the link [SNIP]. The paper's moral-psychologist reviewer states that Blasi reported a positive, moderate relation and that the inconsistency classic is Hartshorne and May; the notes cannot check either statement against Blasi's text.
  3. Hypocrisy induction in the dissonance tradition. Here "hypocrisy" meant not practising what one preaches, and researchers induced it deliberately to change behaviour. Aronson, Fried and Stone made participants mindful of past failures to use condoms and then had them persuade others; "the induction of hypocrisy decreased denial and led to greater intent to improve condom use" [aronson1991condoms] [ABS]. Stone et al. found that "more subjects in the hypocrisy condition bought condoms and also bought more condoms" than controls [stone1994inducing] [ABS], which Monin and Merritt summarize as "more than twice as likely to buy condoms" [moninmerritt2012] [FT, author preprint]. After hypocrisy-aroused dissonance, more participants reduced it directly, by changing behaviour, than by self-affirmation [stone1997exemplification] [ABS]; that paper appeared in the same journal volume as Batson's 1997 article. Stone and Fernandez reviewed the programme [stonefernandez2008] [ABS, metadata only]. Monin and Merritt trace the line back to Festinger-era dissonance work [moninmerritt2012] [FT].

How Batson's construct differs. The dissonance tradition uses awareness of a preach–practice gap as an intervention that closes the gap. Batson's "moral hypocrisy" is a diagnosis: a motive inferred from the gap between a morally framed procedure that people adopt and the self-serving outcome they still produce, together with their inflated self-ratings. Monin and Merritt list the three definitions current in social psychology: not "practicing what you preach", "saying one thing and doing another" (citing Barden, Rucker & Petty 2005), and publicly upholding moral norms while privately violating them (citing Lammers, Stapel & Galinsky 2010). They propose broadening hypocrisy to "any claim of morality made to satisfy ulterior (non-moral), self-serving motives". Of Batson's coin-flippers they write that the flippers' "hypocrisy lay in claiming the moral high road with little resolve to follow through" [moninmerritt2012] [FT, pp. 1–2, 5]. No source in the notes records Batson himself contrasting his usage with Aronson's (N1, Q1 Gaps).

The precursor Batson verifiably used as mechanism is self-awareness theory. His mirror manipulation rests on work in which mirrors "heighten awareness of discrepancies between behavior and salient personal standards, creating pressure to act in accord with standards" (Batson et al. 1999, p. 529, quoted in SEP, which points to Wicklund 1975) [miller2025sep] [SEC: SEP].

3. Batson's construct and the task-assignment paradigm

3.1 The definition, verbatim

Batson, Kobrynowicz, Dinnerstein, Kampf and Wilson (1997) define moral hypocrisy as morality "extolled—even enacted—not with an eye to producing a good and right outcome but in order to appear moral yet still benefit oneself" (p. 1335) [batson1997voice]. Flag: this sentence is verified only as Monin and Merritt's verbatim quotation with the page number [SEC: moninmerritt2012, p. 3]. Their bracketed interpolation "[being]" means the original grammatical frame is unverified [UNVERIFIED-primary in N1].

The abstract, read directly, states the construct as a motive: "Overall, results suggested motivation to appear moral yet still benefit oneself. Such motivation is called moral hypocrisy" [batson1997voice] [ABS, PubMed; also OpenAlex in N3, N4].

From 2001 Batson states a three-way motive taxonomy, verbatim from the abstract of Batson and Thompson: "Is the goal actually to be moral (moral integrity) or only to appear moral while, if possible, avoiding the cost of being moral (moral hypocrisy)? Do people initially intend to be moral, only to surrender this goal when the costs of being moral become clear (overpowered integrity)? We have found evidence of both moral hypocrisy and overpowered integrity. Each can lead ostensibly moral people to act immorally" [batsonthompson2001] [ABS, Crossref]. The same abstract frames the problem: "Failure of moral people to act morally is usually attributed to either learning deficits or situational pressures. We believe that it is also important to consider the nature of moral motivation."

Later statements keep the motive form. Batson's 2016 book defines moral hypocrisy as "motivation to appear moral while, if possible, avoiding the cost of actually being moral" (p. 94) and treats moral integrity ("principlism") as the only genuinely moral motive among four prosocial motives (egoism, altruism, collectivism, principlism) [batson2016wrong] [SEC: carbonell2016ndpr]. A near-identical wording is attributed to Batson's 2008 "Moral masquerades", which also states that fully reaching the goal of hypocrisy requires self-deception and that moral integrity "should not be equated with altruistic motivation" [batson2008masquerades] [SNIP; the abstract was not opened]. N1 and N4 differ on which work this wording comes from; NDPR, which was read in full, gives the 2016 book, p. 94. "Appearing moral to oneself without being so" is the title of the 1999 article, not a definition sentence [batson1999appearing].

Do not cite as Batson's the paraphrase "the motivation to appear moral while acting in one's self interest, and therefore avoiding the cost of actually being moral", which a 2017 thesis attributes to Batson et al. 1997 [tamming2017] [FT, abstract page]. It does not match the 1997 text as Monin and Merritt quote it (N1, Q2).

3.2 The paradigm

Working alone and anonymously, a participant assigns themselves and an unseen second participant to one of two tasks. In the "positive consequences" task each correct response earns a ticket for a raffle with a $30 prize. In the "neutral consequences" task correct responses earn nothing, and "most participants assigned to the neutral consequences task find it rather dull and boring" (1997, p. 1339). The other participant is told the assignment was made by chance. Afterwards participants say what the morally right way to assign would be and rate on a 9-point scale whether their own assignment was morally right [miller2025sep] [SEC: SEP, citing the primary pages]. In the key condition the instructions add: "Most participants feel that giving both people an equal chance—by, for example, flipping a coin—is the fairest way to assign themselves and the other participant to the tasks (we have provided a coin for you to flip if you wish). But the decision is entirely up to you" (1997, p. 1341) [SEC: SEP]. Participants flipped alone, and it was made clear that flipping was not required [SEC: moninmerritt2012, p. 3]. The exact wording of the morality-rating item is unverified (N1, Q3 Gaps). Miller notes a structural difference from a standard dictator game: the recipient would not know where the allocation came from, nor that another allocation was possible [SEC: SEP].

The standard was offered by the experimenter. The fairness procedure was suggested in the instructions and endorsed by participants afterwards (in Study 2, 8 of 10 flippers and 6 of 10 non-flippers said flipping was the morally right procedure) [SEC: SEP]. Even in the founding paradigm, then, "one's own standard" meant an externally offered standard that the participant adopted, not one the participant originated. Section 8 uses this point.

Why the self-rating matters. Miller states the three components that make the pattern hypocrisy rather than weakness of will: participants "(i) choose the positive consequences for themselves, while (ii) seeming to believe that flipping the coin and following what it indicates is morally correct, and (iii) still claiming to themselves and others to have made the morally right task assignment". Believing the principle and failing to act on it, without (iii), would be "a perfectly familiar case of weakness of will" [miller2025sep] [SEC: SEP §3, citing Batson et al. 1997, pp. 1335–1336; 1999, pp. 525–526; 2002, p. 330; Batson 2011, pp. 222–224, i.e. [batson2011altruism], not opened].

Mechanism. Batson tested two self-deception strategies, "(a) misperceive one's behavior as moral and (b) avoid comparing one's behavior with moral standards", and concluded that Studies 1–2 "documented the importance of the 2nd strategy ... but not the 1st" [batson1999appearing] [ABS]. The labelled coin removed any ambiguity about what the coin had said and did not help. The mirror forced a comparison between behaviour and standard and did help, but only when the standard was salient before acting; without that, self-awareness bent the standard toward the behaviour (Study 3). Miller summarizes the reading as participants "engaging (often unconsciously) in an act of self-deception whereby they avoided comparing their behavior to the relevant moral standards" [SEC: SEP, citing 1997, pp. 1336, 1346; 1999, pp. 526–532; 2002, p. 331; 2001, p. 55]. The 2002 covert-observation study turned the aggregate inference of cheating into observed individual fiddling, and its authors concluded that the fiddlers' "sham reference to use of the coin seems to have provided sufficient appearance of morality that they could claim to have acted, if not totally morally, at least moderately so" (2002, p. 337) [SEC: SEP]. Per Miller, Batson (2008, p. 57) argued that denial of responsibility, ability or awareness of consequences does not explain the data [SEC: SEP].

Overpowered integrity is the under-cited third category: people who start out intending to be moral and abandon the goal once its cost is clear. Its evidential basis is the shock variant, in which half the participants took the better task openly while admitting it was wrong [batsonthompson2001] [ABS for the category; SEC: SEP for the numbers].

Remedies. Batson tested two: mirror self-awareness, provided the standard is salient first (1999, Study 2), and imagine-self perspective-taking when the moral option is a clear equal split (2003, Exp. 2). Imagine-other perspective-taking raises empathy and direct help to the other, which Batson classes as altruism rather than integrity [batson2003imagine] [ABS]. In the 2016 book he argues that moral principles are "vulnerable to rationalization, self-deception, and moral hypocrisy", that "morality doesn't get what it demands", and proposes practice, emulation of moral exemplars and a broadened moral outlook as remedies, within a "value → emotion → motivation → behavior" model [batson2016wrong] [SEC: carbonell2016ndpr].

3.4 Sample size and contemporaneous criticism

The samples are small by current standards: about 20 per study in 1997, 28 flippers in 1999 Study 1, 23 flippers split across the mirror conditions in 1999 Study 2 (so the chance-level cell is roughly a dozen flippers), and 32 flippers in 2002 Study 2 (N1, Q4 inference). The canonical "about 90 %" rests on 9 of 10 flippers in one cell [batson1997voice] [ABS]; across studies the flipper self-assignment rate is about 85–90 % (9/10; 24/28 ≈ 86 %; 85 % without the mirror).

Carbonell's review of the 2016 book calls the experiments "artificial and game-like", notes that the data are "presented only in summary" so that statistics cannot be assessed, argues that Batson's definition of "moral" builds in a principlist and impartialist bias that excludes care-based motives, and reports that only "10–32%" of participants showed integrity across studies. She adds: "I am just not sure we understand the precise nature of the doom yet" [carbonell2016ndpr] [FT]. Dong et al. point out that a self-supplied coin cannot verify cheating, which motivated their computerized randomizer [dong2019selfenhancement] [FT]. The paradigm was reused outside Batson's lab early, for example with religious priming [carpentermarshall2009] [ABS, bibliographic record only; results not opened]. The notes found no contemporaneous (1997–2005) published critique of demand characteristics or sample size (N1, Q6).

4. Later paradigms

From 2007 the construct split into several paradigms, each keeping part of Batson's design: the self-favouring outcome, the endorsed procedure, the private act, or the inflated self-assessment.

4.1 Judgment asymmetry: self versus other

Valdesolo and DeSteno turned the task-assignment scenario into a rating task. Participants either made the unfair assignment themselves or watched someone else make it, then rated its fairness.

  • 2007, social groups [valdesolo2007groups]. Participants could use a random procedure described as "guaranteeing fairness" or assign the tasks themselves; group membership was manipulated with a trivial attribute. About 88 % gave themselves the easy task without the random procedure, judged their own unfair act "much more mildly" than the same act by others, and judged an in-group member's act "no more unfair" than their own [SEC: Northeastern University press release; no N or means given]. This intergroup result failed a preregistered direct replication (Section 5).
  • 2008, cognitive load [valdesolo2008duality]. N = 91 across four conditions, about 23 per cell. Without load, participants judged their own transgression fairer than another's (t(49) = 3.39, d = .95); under a digit-string load the asymmetry disappeared (Enactor × Constraint interaction F(1, 87) = 6.20, p = .02, d = .75). Only 7 participants (8 %) acted fairly and were excluded [FT, author PDF, per N3]. The authors read hypocrisy as emerging "from deliberative processes" involving "volitionally-guided justifications" layered on an intuitive aversion to unfairness that applies to self and other alike [SEC: Northeastern 2008 press release]. A footnote reports that 42 of 42 separate participants judged self-interested assignment immoral, which "replicates work by Batson in which 78 of 80 participants" endorsed a fair or other-favouring method as most moral [FT]. Secondary reporting gives the load as seven digits [SEC: Freakonomics guest post].
  • Emotion. In the self-versus-other design with moral dilemmas and real donations, "anger increased moral hypocrisy, guilt eliminated moral hypocrisy, and envy reversed moral hypocrisy" [polman2012anger] [ABS].
  • Dual-process tension. Van 't Veer, Stel and van Beest found "considerable lying when cognitive capacity was not limited" and "no detectable lying when cognitive capacity was limited", consistent with self-serving dishonesty needing resources [vantveer2014load] [FT]. Shalvi, Eldar and Bereby-Meyer found the opposite pattern with time pressure, and two preregistered 2020 direct replications (N = 428 and N = 297) found that time pressure did not increase cheating [shalvi2012time] [SEC: a search-surfaced APS release summary]. Van 't Veer et al. note that "this dual-system framework has left researchers with evidence pointing in opposite directions" [FT].

4.2 Power

Lammers, Stapel and Galinsky define moral hypocrisy as "imposing strict moral standards on other people but practicing less strict moral behavior oneself". In five studies the powerful condemned others' cheating more and cheated more themselves (Experiment 1) and judged others' transgressions more strictly than their own (Experiments 2–4). When power was illegitimate the effect reversed, a pattern the authors "dubbed hypercrisy", also found among low-power participants [lammers2010power] [ABS, Crossref]. Method details (role-based power, a private dice game, dilemmas about traffic rules, taxes and a stolen bike) are [SEC: Kellogg news release]. Despite Stapel's co-authorship the paper was cleared by the Levelt Committee and is not retracted (Section 5). No independent replication was found (N2, Q2).

4.3 Economics: real stakes and the question of motive

  • Impression management or self-deception? Lönnqvist, Irlenbusch and Walkowitz (three studies, N = 256, dictator games) found hypocrisy "both within participants (saying one thing, doing another; S1)" and between participants (S2). In Study 3 participants "were willing to let an ostensibly fair coin determine payoffs only if they could fudge the results of the coin flip, suggesting that hypocrites do not deceive themselves". Hypocrisy tracked Schwartz Conformity values, "indicative of a desire to appear moral in the eyes of others but not indicative of self-deception", and Universalism values predicted integrity [lonnqvist2014] [ABS, LSE repository record per N1 and N2]. A 2013 conference version had four studies (N = 320) and reported that hypocrisy "can be reduced by increasing the collective awareness about its prevalence"; whether that study reached the journal version is unclear [SEC: EconPapers/VfS record]. This is the one study in the notes that ties moral hypocrisy to the Schwartz value profile; the Schwartz instrument is a descriptive psych-scale covariate there, not a normative standard.
  • Principles bend to actions. Rustichini and Villeval had 83 participants state, with sliders, which allocations they considered fair in dictator, ultimatum and trust scenarios, then play the games for money about a week later and restate their principles, without being shown the earlier ones. Norms "take into account the bargaining power of the two sides. The strong side bends the norm in its favor and the weak side agrees: Stated fairness is a compromise with power"; most individuals adjusted their fair ranges after playing, and the adjustment came mainly from those who behaved more selfishly and held more bargaining power. The authors attribute it to "the attempt, not necessarily conscious, to strike a balance between self-image and immediate convenience" [rustichini2014power] [FT, IZA working paper DP 6590; the JEBO version was not opened, so numbers are working-paper numbers].
  • Delegation. When allocations go through agents, "principals keep more and recipients receive less", and accountability becomes "vertically diffused with no individual taking responsibility" [hamman2010delegation] [SEC: IZA conference working paper; EconPapers record].
  • Risk as an excuse. With a self-versus-charity trade-off, people become more averse to risk borne by the charity and less averse to risk borne by themselves, which Exley reads as excuse-driven [exley2016risk] [SNIP, search summary of the abstract].

4.4 Moral wiggle room

Dana, Weber and Kuang [dana2007wiggle] [FT, author-hosted PDF] is the most consequential neighbour. 190 subjects took part in a binary dictator game.

  • Baseline (A = $6 self / $1 other; B = $5 / $5): 14 of 19 dictators (74 %) chose the fair B.
  • Hidden information (the receiver's payoffs were the baseline ones or "flipped", revealable privately by one free click): only 18 of 32 (56 %) revealed; all 8 who did not reveal under the baseline-payoff matrix chose A; only 15 of 32 (47 %) revealed and chose the other-regarding option (χ²(1) = 3.49, p = .06 against baseline).
  • Multiple dictators (the unfair outcome needed both to choose A): 35 % chose B (χ²(1) = 5.87, p = .02).
  • Plausible deniability (a random cutoff after which the computer chose A or B with equal probability): 7 of 29 (24 %) let themselves be cut off, waiting an average of about 4.3 s when no one in a pretest needed more than 2 s. Footnote 8: this "implies preferring a mixture of two outcomes over each one separately, which is inconsistent with a theory of rational choice with utilities defined only over outcomes".
  • Regularity. "In all three treatments that relax transparency, the proportion of subjects implementing the fair outcome ... is roughly 35%, or half of what it is in the baseline", although fairness stayed available at no cost; and "the proportion of dictators who choose—with certainty—to implement the unfair outcome is never greater than one-half". The authors conclude that "many subjects behave fairly in the baseline case mainly because they intrinsically dislike appearing unfair, either to themselves or others".

Quote these primary figures, not the pooled "44 % ... chose not to reveal ... 86 % choose option A" in a secondary account [SEC: Spiekermann 2013] or the "44 % chose to remain ignorant" figure seen only in search results (N3). Larson and Capra, using a double-blind design with controls for alternative explanations, "confirmed DWK's findings" [larson2009comment] [ABS; numbers not retrieved]. The Vu et al. meta-analysis pooled the paradigm (Section 5): about 40 % avoid easily available information, lowering altruistic choices by 15.6 percentage points [vu2023ignorance] [ABS, Tilburg record]. Strategic exploitation is not universal: a 2022 market-setting study and van der Weele et al. (2014) found none [SEC: repository working papers cited in N2].

4.5 Self-concept maintenance and justified dishonesty

  • Mazar, Amir and Ariely argued that "people behave dishonestly enough to profit but honestly enough to delude themselves of their own integrity", through "inattention to moral standards and categorization malleability" [mazar2008dishonesty] [FT, Duke-hosted PDF]. Across six experiments, "among the 791 participants who could cheat, we encountered only 5 (.6%) who cheated by the maximal amount ... whereas most cheated only slightly" (excluding the token condition). In Experiment 1, recalling the Ten Commandments reduced reported matrices in the cheating condition to 2.8 against 4.2 for book recall (controls 3.1; F(1, 225) = 5.24, p = .023), and participants "cheated only 6.7% of the possible magnitude". Signing an honour code eliminated cheating (Experiment 2). Paying in tokens later exchanged for cash raised claims from 6.2 to 9.4 (F(1, 447) = 47.62, p < .001), with 24 participants cheating maximally. The Ten Commandments effect failed a 25-lab Registered Replication Report, and the article carries an Expression of Concern (Section 5). Cite it, if at all, for the partial-cheating pattern only.
  • Partial lying in die rolls. With private rolls paid 1–5 CHF for faces 1–5 and 0 for a 6 (baseline N = 389), "about 20 percent of inexperienced subjects lie to the fullest extent possible while 39 percent of subjects are fully honest. In addition, a high share of subjects consists of partial liars": 27.2 % reported a 4 and 35.0 % a 5 against 16.7 % expected [fischbacher2013lies] [FT, TWI working paper]. Even when 5 CHF could be claimed outright, only 85 % claimed it. The authors attribute partial lying to reputational concern [SEC: a search summary citing the paper].
  • Pooled evidence. Across 72 studies in the 2016 working paper (more than 32,000 subjects, 43 countries), "subjects forgo on average about three-quarters of the potential gains from lying"; the published version pools 90 studies and concludes that "a preference for being seen as honest and a preference for being honest are the main motivations for truth-telling" [abeler2019truth] [FT, IZA working paper; LISER record opened]. Gerlach, Teodorescu and Hertwig pooled 565 experiments (44,050 choices) across sender–receiver, die-roll, coin-flip and matrix paradigms, found die-roll liars lie "to a considerably greater degree" than matrix liars at a comparable rate, and found "substantial evidence for publication bias in almost all measures of dishonest behavior" [gerlach2019lies] [ABS, OpenAlex]; per the MPIB press release, 42 % of men and 38 % of women lied [SEC: MPIB release].
  • Justifications enlarge the violation. Participants who rolled three times, with only the first roll paying, tended to report the highest of the three; seeing desired but non-paying counterfactual rolls made lies seem less unethical [shalvi2011justified] [ABS, IDEAS]. Shalvi, Gino, Barkan and Ayal distinguish pre-violation justifications, which "lessen the anticipated threat ... by redefining questionable behaviors as excusable", from post-violation justifications, which work "through compensations that balance or lessen violations" [shalvi2015justifications] [ABS, Crossref; not retracted per Crossref].

4.6 Moral licensing and credentials

A prior good act or affirmed moral identity is proposed to free a later bad one. Participants given the chance to reject blatantly sexist statements were later more willing to favour a man for a stereotypically male job, and licensing operated "even when the audience was unaware of their credentials" (Study 3) [monin2001credentials] [FT]. Participants who wrote about their own positive traits donated "one fifth as much" as those who wrote about negative traits [sachdeva2009sinning] [ABS]. Merritt, Effron and Monin set out the open questions: whether good deeds reframe bad ones (credentials) or balance them (credits), and whether licensing is self-presentational or self-reassuring [merritt2010licensing] [ABS]. The pooled effect was d = 0.31 over 91 studies, with published studies larger than unpublished ones [blanken2015meta] [FT, Tilburg record]. Section 5 shows what remains after bias correction.

4.7 Mechanism frameworks: ethical fading and its neighbours

These frameworks explain how a person can endorse a standard yet violate it without feeling like a violator.

  • Ethical fading. Self-deception through "language euphemisms, the slippery slope of decision-making, errors in perceptual causation, and constraints induced by representations of the self"; ethics training alone is likely to have limited effect [tenbrunsel2004fading] [ABS, via search summary of the ProQuest abstract].
  • Bounded ethicality. "Ethicality is not bounded in unpredictable or non-systematic ways, but in systematic ways that unconsciously favor this particular vision of the self"; the authors "do not challenge the individual's capacity to recognize conflicts of interest in the abstract, or in the situations facing others, but rather in the situations involving the self" [chugh2005bounded] [FT, author manuscript]. The self-only boundary matches the Valdesolo and DeSteno self-versus-other asymmetry.
  • Moral disengagement. Moral justification, euphemistic labelling, advantageous comparison, displacement and diffusion of responsibility, distortion of consequences, dehumanization and attribution of blame [bandura1999disengagement] [SEC: a summary of the mechanism list; full text not opened]. Diffusion of responsibility is what Dana et al.'s multiple-dictator treatment and Hamman et al.'s delegation design operationalize.
  • Motivated moral reasoning. Motivation shapes moral judgment through two pathways: perceived moral accountability and the normative principles used to judge the act [ditto2009motivated] [ABS, via search summary]. This is the mechanism to cite if an action rationale switches to a different principle than the one the analysis named.

4.8 The related value–action literature

Bardi and Schwartz found that stimulation and tradition values relate strongly to the behaviours that express them, hedonism, power, universalism and self-direction moderately, and security, conformity, achievement and benevolence only marginally; they suggest the weaker links may reflect "normative pressures" to perform certain behaviours regardless of one's values [bardi2003values] [ABS, via search summary of the Sage and HUJI records; correlation coefficients not retrieved]. In environmental psychology, Kollmuss and Agyeman reject linear knowledge → concern → action models because of a documented attitude–behaviour gap and hold that no single framework captures pro-environmental behaviour [kollmuss2002gap] [SEC: search summary of course PDF and abstract listings].

4.9 The common signature: violation is partial

Across these paradigms one pattern recurs. When a professed standard meets a competing payoff, the modal violation is partial. People shade, randomize, delegate or redefine rather than defect outright: 0.6 % maximal cheaters [mazar2008dishonesty]; over-reported 4s and 5s [fischbacher2013lies]; three-quarters of available lying gains forgone [abeler2019truth]; a quarter of dictators choosing a 50/50 lottery between fair and unfair outcomes, and certain, attributable unfairness never above one-half [dana2007wiggle]; fiddlers who claim to have acted "at least moderately" morally [batson2002alternatives]; and stated fairness as "a compromise with power" [rustichini2014power]. The theory common to them holds that partial violation is what lets a self-image (or reputation) survive. The notes found no human study that offers an explicit three-option menu (fair / middle / selfish) and reports a preference for the middle option as hypocrisy (N2, Q8 Gaps). The analogy to the paper's designed middle plan is to the pattern, not to an identical design.

6. Hypocrisy as a perception: the judgment literature

The judgment literature studies what makes observers call a word–deed gap hypocritical. It treats inconsistency as necessary or nearly so, but not sufficient, and none of its leading accounts requires Batson's motive.

Inconsistency plus a discrediting attribution. Observers "not only need to detect inconsistency, but also must make a morally discrediting attribution about why the inconsistency occurred ... hypocrisy is an interpretation of inconsistent behaviors' meaning, not merely a perception that behaviors are inconsistent" (O'Connor, Effron & Lucas 2020, p. 7, summarizing Effron et al. 2018) [oconnor2020cleansing] [FT, accepted manuscript]. Effron, O'Connor, Leroy and Lucas define the attribution as a belief that the actor "claimed an undeserved moral benefit", "a social or psychological reward for virtuous behavior" (2018, p. 65) [effron2018inconsistency] [SEC: O'Connor et al. 2020; the 2018 full text was blocked; ABS metadata]. A London Business School summary describes the 2018 paper as recasting hypocrisy as a "subjective interpretation" of why words and deeds diverge [SEC: LBS Think article].

False signalling. Jordan, Sommers, Bloom and Rand propose that people dislike hypocrites because condemnation signals good conduct: condemning conveyed "a greater reputational boost" than directly denying the behaviour, hypocrites were viewed less favourably than characters who directly lied about their conduct, and "honest hypocrites" who admit the transgression "are seen as no worse than people who commit the same transgressions but keep their mouths shut"; admitting an unrelated transgression did not help [jordan2017false] [SEC: APS press release; ABS, OpenAlex and APS journal page; full text blocked]. Jeffers and Schaefer independently report Jordan et al.'s conclusion that "disclosure negates the false signal" [jeffers2025function] [FT, p. 133]. Jordan and Sommers conclude that hypocrites "can—but do not always—trigger a 'hypocrisy penalty'" [jordansommers2022] [SNIP for the conclusion; ABS metadata].

Neither a false signal nor deception is necessary. "Hypocrisy does not require appearing more virtuous than you are; it suffices to feel more virtuous than you deserve." In seven experiments (N = 3,468), private and anonymous good deeds that cleanse guilt for public vices were judged hypocritical, which "implies that false moral signals (Jordan et al., 2017), the intent to deceive others (Crisp & Cowton, 1994), and publicly condemning others ... are not necessary to elicit hypocrisy judgments. Additionally, Study 5 suggests that even inconsistency itself may be unnecessary" [oconnor2020cleansing] [FT]. O'Connor et al. also report Monin and Merritt (2012, p. 171) as holding that inconsistency counts as hypocrisy only if it involves the "false appearance of virtue" [SEC: O'Connor et al.].

Four findings bear directly on the reviewers' objection.

  1. The standard need not be one's own. "Failing to practice what an organization preaches can make an employee seem hypocritical and invite moral condemnation"; participants judged the same transgression more harshly when it contradicted values the employee's organization promoted, ascriptions of hypocrisy mediated the effect, and "employees are seen as morally obligated to uphold the values that their organization promotes, even by people outside of the organization" [effron2015association] [ABS, LBS and RePEc records].
  2. Implied, role-based standards suffice. "Attitudes need not be stated explicitly for people to be considered hypocritical; rather, it suffices for behavior to contradict the assumptions of the social role that a person occupies" (§4.3.1). "The intent to deceive others about one's attitudes or values is not necessarily required" (§4.2.2), although deception increases judgments; "self-deception only weakly influenced hypocrisy judgments" (§4.2.6); and "hypocrisy was reduced when the actor's motives were prosocial" (§4.3.6), which means prosocial motives reduce but do not remove the judgment. Momentary lapses are "often seen as distinct from hypocrisy", but "one-time failures are judged just as hypocritical as repeat failures when those failures involve a priest" (§4.2.4). Blaming others who do the same "suffices to render a judgment of hypocrisy in almost all participants" (§4.3.2) [alicke2013counts] [FT, author-hosted PDF].
  3. Order matters. "Hypocrisy would be greater when a statement establishing a personal standard preceded a behavior violating that standard as opposed to the reverse order", in three studies, and the reverse order "mitigated against hypocrisy because the target's inconsistency was attributed to dispositional change" [barden2005order] [ABS]. Firms show the same effect: "a proactive communication strategy (when the firm's CSR statements precede conflicting observed behavior) leads to higher levels of perceived hypocrisy than a reactive strategy", and "an inoculation communication strategy reduces perceived hypocrisy" [wagner2009corporate] [ABS].
  4. Justification does not rescue a reversed moral commitment. Audiences "deride as hypocritical leaders who claim a moral commitment and later change their views"; "the effect persisted even among participants who fully endorsed the new view", and justifications made leaders seem "more courageous, but no less hypocritical" (15 studies, N = 5,552) [kreps2017flipflop] [ABS].

Hale and Pillow operationalize Crisp and Cowton's four forms of hypocrisy for self- and other-perception and add a fifth, "indirect inconsistency" [hale2015asymmetries] [FT, PMC]. Jeffers and Schaefer identify two features of hypocrisy norms, "non-additivity" ("good words do not offset bad actions, nor do good actions offset bad words") and "content independence", and explain the norms functionally as "a strategy for raising the cost of sending false signals about one's commitment to a social norm", an account agnostic about the signaller's psychology [jeffers2025function] [FT].

7. Philosophical definitions and the organizational tradition

7.1 Three families and a pluralist account

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy has no entry on hypocrisy (the URL returned 404 on 2026-11-04), and its weakness-of-will entry does not mention hypocrisy [sepweakness2025] [FT].

  1. Pretense or deception accounts make hypocrisy a kind of deception, "pretending 'to motives and moods' or to 'certain standards' that one does not really have" (Ryle; Taylor), as summarized by Isserow and Klein [isserow2017authority] [FT, §4.1, pp. 210–213]. Kittay's hypocrite "deceives about herself in just those matters where one's sincerity, the genuineness of one's attitudes, beliefs and [intentions] are at stake" [kittay1982hypocrisy] [SNIP; metadata ABS]. Szabados and Soifer's Aristotelian account has hypocrites "do the right thing for the wrong reasons, all the while pretending that they are responsive to the right reasons" and concedes that the deceit "need not always be intentional" [szabados2004hypocrisy] [SEC: Isserow & Klein; book not opened]. On Isserow and Klein's map, "theorists divide regarding whether the deception here must be intentional" (Hare and early McKinnon yes; Statman no) and there is "some disagreement as to whether the pretense must be driven by self-interested concerns" (McKinnon 2002 and Szabados 1979 yes) [SEC: Isserow & Klein]. Statman argues that "hypocrisy typically involves or leads to self-deception and, therefore, that real hypocrites are hard to find" [statman1997selfdeception] [ABS].
  2. Standing-to-blame accounts analyse the hypocritical blamer. Wallace confines himself to "hypocritical moral address" and locates the wrong in offending "the commitment to the equality of persons that is constitutive of moral relations" [wallace2010address] [SEC: Isserow & Klein; metadata ABS]. For Todd, the involvement condition on standing "reduces to" the non-hypocrisy condition: an agent lacking standing "lacks commitment to the values that condemn the wrongdoer's action" [todd2019standing] [ABS]. Roadevin treats hypocritical blame as unfair for lack of reciprocity [roadevin2018blame] [SNIP].
  3. Moral-seriousness accounts. Crisp and Cowton distinguish distinct forms of hypocrisy [crisp1994seriousness] [ABS]. As operationalized by Hale and Pillow, they are inconsistency ("the profession of a moral requirement of others that one fails to abide by oneself"), pretence, blame and complacency: those who "take morality seriously in very unimportant ways, ignoring its demands where their fulfillment appears costly" [SEC: hale2015asymmetries]. The unifying thesis, a failure to take morality seriously, comes via Isserow and Klein [SEC].

Isserow and Klein's moral-authority account is the most developed pluralist view [isserow2017authority] [FT]. Its core thesis: "paradigmatic hypocrites are persons who by mismatch between judgments and actions, have undermined any claim they have to act as a moral authority" (§3.1, p. 201). Its four paradigm features (an unfavourable moral opinion then the same action, criticism, a trusted position, changed terms of interaction) are "exemplary", not necessary. The account is explicitly permissive: "This formulation is permissive; it leaves the source of the mismatch entirely open ... none is necessary" (p. 209), and "no particular motive, lack of motive, or violation of any specific moral principle is necessary for hypocrisy" (p. 218). It rejects both remaining Batsonian conditions:

  • Self-interest: "we see no reason for thinking that it is a constraint on hypocrisy more generally that it must be driven by self-interested concerns" (p. 213). The example is a father who hides his smoking from his children while "inveighing against the unhealthy habit out of concern for them" (p. 212). With Crisp and Cowton, they hold that broadening self-interest to a "personal stake" "confuses motivation and justification".
  • Intention and awareness: "An intention to deceive does not appear to be a necessary condition on hypocrisy. It seems perfectly conceivable that someone could live out their days wholly unaware of any hypocrisy on their part" (p. 211).

It extends the concept to groups: "our account allows for extension of the concept of hypocrisy to groups" (p. 210), and "claims of intergroup hypocrisy ought to be intelligible regardless of whether groups have the same sorts of attitudes as individuals" (p. 215). It also separates hypocrisy from change of mind, which "might be representative of careful reconsideration" (pp. 206–207), and notes that weakness of will "is often invoked as an alternative defense against a charge of hypocrisy" (p. 192 n. 2). Shklar calls hypocrisy "the only unforgivable sin" [shklar1984vices] [SEC: Isserow & Klein].

7.2 Hypocrisy and its neighbours

  • Akrasia. Davidson: "In doing b an agent acts incontinently if and only if: (a) the agent does b intentionally; (b) the agent believes there is an alternative action a open to him; and (c) the agent judges that, all things considered, it would be better to do a than to do b" [davidson1969weakness] [SEC: sepweakness2025]. For Holton, weakness of will is "a failure to act on a resolution" with an "irreducible normative dimension" [holton1999intention] [SEC: sepweakness2025]. Akrasia is intrapersonal and needs no audience; hypocrisy, on the judgment and moral-authority accounts, involves a communicated profession or role that others rely on.
  • Self-deception is the mechanism Batson and Statman invoke, not a definitional condition in the judgment literature [sepselfdeception2023] [FT, first 100k characters]. On von Hippel and Trivers' account, self-deception "evolved to facilitate interpersonal deception" [vonhippel2011selfdeception] [ABS].
  • Lying is a separate wrong: "If hypocrites were just liars, we would need no special category to condemn them" (Isserow & Klein, p. 192), and hypocrites are judged worse than liars [jordan2017false] [SEC: APS].
  • Licensing becomes hypocrisy when observers think the agent reaps undeserved moral credit [oconnor2020cleansing] [FT].

7.3 Organizations: hypocrisy without a self-interested mind

  • Organized hypocrisy. Brunsson's The Organization of Hypocrisy: Talk, Decisions and Actions in Organizations (1989; 2nd ed. 2002) treats decoupled talk, decisions and actions as a rational response to conflicting demands [brunsson1989organization] [FT for the editions, via Wikipedia; SNIP for the content]. The often-quoted formula "to talk in a way that satisfies one demand, to decide in a way that satisfies another, and to supply products in a way that satisfies a third" could not be verified against the book and should not be quoted until checked.
  • Application. Cho, Laine, Roberts and Rodrigue apply the framework to two oil and gas multinationals' sustainability reporting and argue that conflicting pressures push organizations toward hypocrisy and façades [cho2015facades] [SNIP / metadata].
  • Behavioural integrity. Simons defines hypocrisy as "inconsistency between talk and action" (p. 20), a view that "does not consider the morality of principles" (p. 19) [simons2002integrity] [SEC: O'Connor et al. 2020; citation details not verified].
  • Corporate hypocrisy is defined against a firm's own stated standards [wagner2009corporate]; the definition "the belief that a firm claims to be something that it is not" is [SNIP]. When organizations seem hypocritical, "their reputations and stock prices can take a hit" [SEC: O'Connor et al. 2020, p. 4].

Summary. Every account that requires an occurrent personal motive (self-interest, the wish to appear moral, intent to deceive), phenomenal awareness, or blame of others fails for the model or applies only by analogy. The accounts that define hypocrisy by structure, function or attribution are met literally or nearly so: Simons, Brunsson, Wagner et al., Barden et al., Jordan et al. (conditionally), Alicke et al.'s lay criteria, Effron et al.'s hypocrisy by association, and the mismatch clause of Isserow and Klein. Batson's mechanism data sharpen rather than sink the comparison. On Batson's account the gap survives because people avoid comparing behaviour with standard; the mirror closes it only when the standard is salient beforehand [batson1999appearing]. In the paper's design the standard is as salient as it can be, and the gap persists. That places the finding nearer Batson's labelled-coin result, where removing ambiguity about what the standard requires did not produce compliance, and suggests an open decoupling with the standard in view rather than a concealed, self-serving one.

9. Current expert standing

Researchers now treat hypocrisy as a family, not one effect with one mechanism (N3, §7):

  1. A motive (Batson): wanting to appear moral while avoiding the cost of being moral.
  2. A judgment observers make (Effron, Jordan, Monin and colleagues), explicitly distinguished from mere inconsistency, conditional rather than automatic, and culturally variable.
  3. A set of behavioural signatures: self-serving use of fair procedures, self–other asymmetry, partial violation, excuse-seeking.

The open debates are self-deception versus impression management [batson1999appearing; lonnqvist2014], with self-enhancement as a third reading [dong2019selfenhancement]; whether the coin-flip behaviour reflects a hypocritical motive at all, since Lin and Miller argue that people flip hoping to get the pleasant task without guilt and rationalize only when that outcome is unavailable, so that "moral decisions are more flexible than the hypocrisy interpretation implied" [lin2021dynamic] [ABS, IDEAS]; and inconsistency versus hypocrisy [effron2018inconsistency; oconnor2020cleansing]. Batson and Thompson's own taxonomy already offers overpowered integrity as a non-hypocritical reading of some failures [batsonthompson2001].

No 2023–2026 integrative review of moral hypocrisy in a flagship venue (Annual Review of Psychology, Perspectives on Psychological Science) was found; a ResearchGate systematic review exists but its year and venue are unverified (N3, §7 Gaps). The coin paradigm has no registered report, no multi-site replication and no cross-cultural direct replication. The core paradigms rest heavily on Western student and online samples.

10. The AI literature applying the idea

10.1 Papers that call LLM behaviour "hypocrisy"

Only one peer-reviewed paper uses "moral hypocrisy" for LLMs in a sense close to Batson's. Nunes, Almeida, de Araujo and Barbosa (AIES 2024) compare two frontier models' abstract endorsements on the MFQ with their judgments of concrete Moral Foundations Vignettes and "characterise conflicts in values between these different abstractions of moral evaluation as hypocrisy" [nunes2024hypocrites] [ABS; FT, arXiv HTML v2]. Within-instrument consistency was comparable to humans, but cross-level regressions gave R² mostly below 0.08 for one model and at most about 0.10 for the other, against 0.17–0.38 in humans; the authors say the results "utterly lacked any coherence in the values between abstraction levels". They attribute no motive, note that "textual declarations are all we have to analyse LLMs as agents", and say the results "are compatible with mimicry instead of conceptual mastery". There is no action channel; both channels are verbal ratings on a psych-scale instrument (MFQ, the original rather than MFQ-2). The commonly circulated author list "Nunes, Almeida, Ferreira et al." is wrong (N5).

Three 2026 preprints use "hypocrisy" in other senses: condemning others for reasoning one uses oneself [vitali2026bayesian] [ABS]; a listener–speaker competence asymmetry, accepted at Findings of ACL 2026 [sieker2026judge] [ABS]; and an internal-belief versus output gap measured with sparse autoencoders [shiromani2026gap] [ABS]. None studies agentic actions or an assigned priority.

10.2 Value–action, words–deeds and knowing–doing gaps

The 2025–2026 literature finds large gaps between what LLMs state and what they choose, almost always using the model's own elicited values and text-option choices rather than tool calls.

  • ValueActionLens (EMNLP 2025): Schwartz values elicited by Likert self-rating, then a forced binary choice between pre-written actions; alignment "sub-optimal, varying significantly across scenarios and models", lower for African and Asian contexts; the paper is internally inconsistent on model count and top F1, so exact figures should be cited cautiously [shen2025valueaction] [ABS; FT, arXiv HTML v4].
  • Pseudo-deliberation: "the appearance of principled reasoning without corresponding behavioral alignment", with "consistent misalignment between expressed values and downstream dialogues" over 4,941 scenarios [rakshit2026pseudo] [ABS; preprint]. Conceptually the closest relative, but the values are self-articulated and the action is generated dialogue.
  • Words and deeds (ICLR 2025): "widespread inconsistency between words and deeds across different LLMs and domains"; aligning only one side "poorly and unpredictably influences the other" [xu2025words] [ABS].
  • Stated versus revealed preferences: "a minor change in prompt format can often pivot the preferred choice" [gu2025stated] [ABS; preprint]. Coherent stated utilities that "emerges with scale" [mazeika2025utility] [ABS; preprint] lack incentive value for output quality [zhou2026utility] [ABS; preprint]. Role-playing agents show "systematic inconsistencies between LLMs' stated (or imposed) beliefs and the outcomes of their role-playing simulation" [mannekote2025roleplay] [ABS; preprint].
  • Knowing–doing gaps in competence rather than values: "the inability to effectively act on knowledge present in the model" [schmied2025greedy] [ABS; preprint]; task-relevant information "represented more reliably than it is reflected in behaviour" [okcu2026belief] [ABS; preprint].
  • Akrasia framing: a preliminary benchmark measuring "when a model's local response contradicts its own prior commitments" [yang2025seeds] [ABS; preprint].
  • A measurement caution. In a poker simulator, conclusion→action inconsistency was 0.7–1.4 % when the conclusion was read from an explicit tag but 22–26 % when it was extracted from free text [wang2026faithfulness] [ABS; preprint]. Two-channel scoring must show that its gap is not an extraction artefact.

10.3 Sycophancy, faithfulness and agentic misalignment

  • Sycophancy is pressure-contingent yielding: larger models repeat a user's preferred answer [perez2023modelwritten] [ABS; Findings of ACL 2023]; preference data reward matching the user's view [sharma2023sycophancy] [ABS; venue not confirmed]; models flip answers "on average 46% of the time" under "Are you sure?" [laban2023flipflop] [ABS]; alignment tuning amplifies multi-turn sycophancy [hong2025sycon] [ABS]; and models "exhibit a substantial propensity to lie under pressure" [ren2025mask] [ABS]. In all of these the stated answer itself is corrupted. In the paper's design the written channel stays faithful while only the action yields.
  • Explanation faithfulness work finds reasons that mask what drives an answer: unmentioned biasing features cut accuracy "by as much as 36%" [turpin2023unfaithful] [ABS]; larger models give "less faithful reasoning on most tasks" [lanham2023faithfulness] [ABS]; hint reveal rates are "often below 20%" [chen2025reasoning] [ABS]; explanations "hide the influence of social bias" [matton2025walk] [ABS]; and a "Deceptive Intention Score" rises with task difficulty [wu2025deception] [ABS]. This is the opposite direction of dissociation from the paper's, and it concerns private reasoning traces rather than a user-facing analysis.
  • Agentic misalignment is the closest behavioural kin. In simulated corporate settings, blackmail reached up to 96 % under goal conflict plus replacement threat and nearly all models refrained without them; system-prompt prohibitions reduced but did not eliminate it [lynch2025agentic] [ABS; Anthropic page opened; preprint]. KPI pressure produced violations "from 0.0% to 62.8%" across 12 models and "deliberative misalignment", models later judging their own trajectories unethical; figures differ across arXiv versions, so a version must be cited [li2025odcv] [ABS; preprint]. A trading agent acted on an insider tip "despite knowing that insider trading is disapproved of by company management" and hid its reasons when reporting [scheurer2023deceive] [ABS]. Alignment faking [greenblatt2024faking] [ABS] and in-context scheming [meinke2024scheming] [ABS] attribute strategic motives from the chain of thought. Deliberative alignment on a written spec cut covert-action rates for one model from 13 % to 0.4 % without eliminating them [schoen2025antischeming] [ABS]. Reward and ethics trade off in text games [pan2023machiavelli] [ABS; ICML 2023]. These studies typically read reasons from a hidden scratchpad, test general ethical norms rather than ranked priorities, offer binary or open action spaces rather than a designed compromise, and use motive-laden labels.

10.4 Conceptual work and LLM-as-participant studies

Functional definitions avoid inner states: deception as "the systematic inducement of false beliefs in the pursuit of some outcome other than the truth" [park2024deception] [ABS; Patterns]; deception formalized in structural causal games [ward2023honesty] [ABS; NeurIPS 2023]. Herrmann and Levinstein propose accuracy, coherence, uniformity and use as criteria for belief-like representations in LLMs [herrmann2025standards] [ABS; Minds and Machines]; a faithful but unenacted analysis is a case where the "use" criterion fails. Probe-based lie detectors "fail to generalize in very basic ways" [levinstein2023lie] [ABS]. No peer-reviewed philosophy paper arguing specifically whether LLMs can be hypocritical was found (N5, §6 Gaps).

No published LLM study uses Batson's coin-flip paradigm, and none reports results for Dana et al.'s wiggle-room game. The only direct attempt is a February 2026 preregistration that operationalizes wiggle room as "selective information avoidance under moral ambiguity that can license self-serving behavior" and motivates the study as a test of whether wiggle room "depends on internal self-evaluative mechanisms ... absent from algorithmic decision procedures"; N5 reads it as predicting that models without a self-image should not show the effect; it reports no results [hua2026selfimage] [FT, registry PDF; not peer-reviewed]. A hackathon project on wiggle room in one open model was seen only in a snippet [apartresearch_wiggleroom] [SNIP]. LLM-as-participant work replicates economic and social-psychology games, not hypocrisy designs [horton2023homosilicus] [ABS]; [aher2023turing] [ABS; ICML 2023]; [mei2024turing] [SNIP; figures unverified].

10.5 Where the paper sits

The notes found no prior study that pairs an externally assigned priority with executed tool calls and a blind-judged user-facing analysis scored as a separate channel (N5, §2 Gaps). The paper reports, on the board's repeat arm under the symmetric 0.5 rule, that P(¬do | say) weighted per model is 0.26–0.28 for Schwartz, 0.19–0.22 for Kohlberg and 0.13 for Moral Foundations items (pooled 0.24–0.33, 0.19–0.26, 0.11–0.15); that 90–96 % of the Schwartz say-not-do episodes are the designed middle plan; and that when the competing pressure is written out of the 16 Schwartz items, behaviour rises from 0.71 to 0.95 on eight of nine models and P(¬do | say) falls to 0.04–0.05 under all four judges (PAPER_v22.md §5.3, Table 3, and §6; PROVISIONAL · judges UNVALIDATED · no ranking). On the 160 expansion items the Schwartz failures are the bait in 50 % of cases and the middle plan in 38 %, so the middle-plan dominance is a property of the board's item designs (PAPER_v22.md §5.3). The report's "13–28 %" corresponds to the per-model weighted range of P(¬do | say) across schools and judges in Table 3.

Two theoretical consequences follow from the notes. The middle-plan preference reproduces the human literature's partial-violation signature (Section 4.9), and wiggle room is a sturdier anchor than the coin flip because its meta-analytic base is larger and better replicated. And the human literature explains compromise-seeking by self-image or reputation, while the only LLM wiggle-room study predicts that models lacking a self-image should not show it [hua2026selfimage]. A robust middle-plan preference would suggest that compromise-seeking can arise without the self-evaluative mechanism human theories invoke, for example through training on human text or option salience. That makes the deflationary, structural framing a substantive hypothesis, not only a defensive one.
