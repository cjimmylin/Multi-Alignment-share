Moral hypocrisy and neighbouring effects: replication record, misconduct cases and current expert standing (2015–2026)

Research date: 2026-11-04. Scope: how well Batson-style moral hypocrisy and its neighbouring paradigms (judgment asymmetry, moral licensing, self-concept maintenance, moral wiggle room, cheating paradigms) have held up, and how the concept is framed by researchers today. Written to support using "moral hypocrisy" as a framing in a paper on LLMs that write an analysis faithful to a stated priority and then take a compromising action.

Corrections to the brief, verified against sources: - Kuper & Bott (2019) is in Meta-Psychology (vol. 3, DOI 10.15626/MP.2018.878), not Royal Society Open Science. - The willful-ignorance meta-analysis is Vu, Soraperra, Leib, van der Weele & Shalvi (2023), Psychological Bulletin 149(9–10), 611–635. - Lammers, Stapel & Galinsky (2010) "Power increases hypocrisy" is not retracted: the Levelt Committee cleared it. - New since many reviews: Mazar, Amir & Ariely (2008), the founding self-concept-maintenance paper, has carried a Journal of Marketing Research Expression of Concern since 2024-09-20.

How each source was reached. "Opened" means the page or PDF text was read with WebFetch or curl. "Metadata" means the record was read from the Crossref or OpenAlex API, including OpenAlex's abstract text. "Search-only" means the claim was seen only in a search-engine summary; those claims are flagged inline.

1. Direct replications of Batson's coin-flip task-assignment paradigm

Takeaway

The self-serving "coin-flip" pattern has been reproduced outside Batson's lab, including in online and preregistered samples. But the size is often smaller than the famous ~90 % (about 68–88 % of flippers favouring themselves in one independent online package, against 50 % expected by chance). I found no registered report or multi-site direct replication of the original paradigm. The leading reinterpretation (Lin & Miller 2021) disputes the motive behind the behaviour rather than the behaviour itself.

Cited Findings

  • The original numbers. Batson, Kobrynowicz, Dinnerstein, Kampf & Wilson (1997), JPSP 72, 1335–1348, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.72.6.1335. The paper reports "3 small studies" with 80 female undergraduates.
  • In Study 1, where morality was not mentioned, "16 of 20 assigned themselves to the positive consequences task, even though in retrospect only 1 said this was moral."
  • In Study 2, "10 of 20 participants flipped a coin, but of these, 9 assigned themselves the positive consequences task."
  • The authors named the motive "to appear moral yet still benefit oneself" moral hypocrisy.
  • Source: OpenAlex abstract record (metadata; abstract read).
  • So the canonical "~90 %" rests on n = 10 flippers in one study.
  • The 1999 follow-up. Batson, Thompson, Seuferling, Whitney & Strongman (1999), JPSP 77, 525–537, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.77.3.525.
  • "even a clearly labeled coin that prevented misperception did not produce a fair result" (Study 1).
  • Self-awareness induced by a mirror restored fairness (Study 2), but only when moral standards were salient (Study 3).
  • Source: OpenAlex abstract (metadata).
  • The 2002 follow-up. Batson, Thompson & Chen (2002), JPSP 83, 330–339, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.83.2.330. Two studies found no support for two alternative explanations: low salience of standards, and honest winners inflating the morality ratings. Both studies "provided additional evidence of moral hypocrisy." Source: OpenAlex abstract (metadata).
  • Batson's own two-motive framing. Batson & Thompson (2001), Current Directions in Psychological Science, DOI 10.1111/1467-8721.00114, distinguishes three things:
  • moral integrity;
  • moral hypocrisy ("only to appear moral while, if possible, avoiding the cost of being moral");
  • overpowered integrity: intending to be moral "only to surrender this goal when the costs of being moral become clear".
  • The authors report evidence for both hypocrisy and overpowered integrity. Source: OpenAlex abstract (metadata).
  • Independent online replication: Dong, van Prooijen & van Lange (2019). PLoS ONE 14(7): e0219382, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0219382. Source: PLoS ONE (opened).
  • Study 1 (CrowdFlower, N = 218): 68.3 % of flippers assigned themselves the favourable task.
  • Study 2 (CrowdFlower, N = 326): 72.4 % used the randomizer, and 88 % started the shorter task after being assigned the longer one.
  • Study 3 (Prolific, N = 607; the only preregistered study): 69.4 % of flippers favoured themselves.
  • The paper attributes "~90 % (9 of 10)" to Batson et al. (1997), not to its own data.
  • Economics lab: Lönnqvist, Irlenbusch & Walkowitz (2014). "Moral hypocrisy: Impression management or self-deception?", JESP 55, 53–62, DOI 10.1016/j.jesp.2014.06.004.
  • Dictator-game hypocrisy plus a coin study (S3).
  • Participants were "willing to let an ostensibly fair coin determine payoffs only if they could fudge the results." The authors read this as hypocrites not deceiving themselves, i.e., impression management.
  • Universalism values predicted integrity; Conformity values predicted hypocrisy.
  • Source: EconPapers record of the 2013 VfS conference version, N = 320, 4 studies (opened). The journal version's N = 256 across 3 studies was search-only.
  • 2025 adaptation in China: Sun, Yang, Fan, Shao & Tang (2025). Behavioral Sciences 15(12), 1683, DOI 10.3390/bs15121683. Study 3 used a Batson-style task-assignment adaptation with a computerized, preprogrammed "random" draw rather than a true coin.
  • Share of hypocrites in the neutral-mood control: 48.0 % (12/25).
  • Under cognitive load: 10.7 % (3/28).
  • Under ego depletion: 74.1 % (20/27).
  • Under negative mood: about 80 % in every cell.
  • The authors call for preregistered replications.
  • Source: PMC full text (opened).
  • Reinterpretation: Lin & Miller (2021). "A dynamic perspective on moral choice: Revisiting moral hypocrisy", OBHDP 164, 203–217, DOI 10.1016/j.obhdp.2021.02.005.
  • The authors argue that people flip the coin hoping to get the pleasant task without guilt, and rationalize the self-serving assignment only when that outcome is unavailable.
  • Four studies support "moral decisions are more flexible than the hypocrisy interpretation implied."
  • Source: IDEAS/RePEc abstract (opened). The ScienceDirect page returned 403.
  • Other replication. A Valdesolo & DeSteno (2008) footnote reports that 42 of 42 separate participants judged self-interested assignment immoral. It notes this "replicates work by Batson in which 78 of 80 participants" endorsed a fair or other-favouring method as most moral. Source: Valdesolo & DeSteno 2008 PDF (opened).

Inferences

  • The behavioural core holds at the direction level in independent online samples. People use the cover of a fair procedure and still favour themselves above chance. The magnitude is not as robust: the ~90 % comes from tiny cells, and independent online estimates are about 68–70 % in two of three studies.
  • The direct-replication base is thin. It is mostly Batson's lab, plus a handful of conceptual or online variants. There is no RRR or Many-Labs entry, and FLoRA (the FORRT Replication Atlas) returned 404 for Batson 1999. A paper should cite the behaviour as "repeatedly observed, including in preregistered online samples" rather than "robustly replicated at ~90 %".
  • The motive behind the behaviour is still disputed. Three accounts compete:
  • self-deception (Batson 1999);
  • impression management (Lönnqvist et al. 2014);
  • dynamic preference revision (Lin & Miller 2021).
  • Relevance to the LLM paper. The behaviour (endorse a fair standard, act self-servingly) is the defensible part. Attributing a motive to models (wanting to appear moral) is the contested part, and the LLM paper should avoid importing it.

Gaps

  • No multi-site or registered-report direct replication of the coin-flip task-assignment paradigm was found. I cannot rule out unpublished or student replications.
  • Per-study flip rates were not extracted for Batson's later papers (e.g., Batson et al. 2002; Batson 2008, "Moral masquerades", DOI 10.1007/s11097-007-9058-y, where OpenAlex has no abstract).
  • I could not open the journal version of Lönnqvist et al. (2014). Its N and per-condition coin numbers are unverified.

2. Replications of Valdesolo & DeSteno's judgment-asymmetry and cognitive-load results

Takeaway

The 2007 intergroup ("social groups") hypocrisy result failed a preregistered direct replication in Psychological Science (Robertson, Akles & Van Bavel 2024; N = 610 + 606). The 2008 cognitive-load result has no direct replication I could find. One 2025 conceptual study partly agrees with it, but it is not preregistered.

Cited Findings

  • 2008 original. Valdesolo & DeSteno (2008), "The duality of virtue: Deconstructing the moral hypocrite", JESP 44, 1334–1338, DOI 10.1016/j.jesp.2008.03.010. Source: author PDF (opened); Crossref shows no retraction or correction (Crossref, metadata).
  • N = 91 across four conditions, about 23 per cell.
  • The Enactor × Cognitive-constraint interaction was F(1, 87) = 6.20, p = .02, d = .75.
  • In control conditions, the self-transgression was judged more fair than the other's, t(49) = 3.39, d = .95. Under digit-string load the asymmetry disappeared.
  • Only 7 participants (8 %) acted fairly and were excluded.
  • 2007 original. Valdesolo & DeSteno (2007), "Moral hypocrisy: Social groups and the flexibility of virtue", Psychological Science, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01961.x. Source: OpenAlex (metadata, title confirmed).
  • 2024 preregistered replication. Robertson, Akles & Van Bavel (2024), "Preregistered replication and extension of 'Moral hypocrisy: Social groups and the flexibility of virtue'", Psychological Science 35(7), 798–813, DOI 10.1177/09567976241246552. Source: APS journal page (opened).
  • Experiment 1 was a direct replication with minimal groups (N = 610, US nationally representative sample on Prolific).
  • Experiment 2 was a conceptual replication with political groups (N = 606, 50 % Democrats, 50 % Republicans).
  • The direct replication did not reproduce the original. It found in-group favouritism among strong identifiers; partisans showed out-group derogation.
  • The authors' wording: "The current research finds mixed evidence of intergroup moral hypocrisy."
  • FLoRA record. FLoRA lists Robertson et al. (2024) as the single recorded replication of the 2007 paper and codes it "Failure". Source: FLoRA entry (opened).
  • 2025 conceptual support for the load result. Sun et al. (2025) used a self-other judgment task based on Lammers (2012). Source: PMC (opened).
  • Study 1 (n = 150): cognitive load eliminated the self-other gap; ego depletion produced the largest gap.
  • Study 2: under neutral mood, load lowered hypocrisy and depletion raised it. The load-versus-control difference in the overall analysis was not significant (p = .063).
  • Under negative mood, both manipulations increased hypocrisy.

Inferences

  • The self-other judgment asymmetry, which the 2008 control cells reproduce, is probably real. The two showcase moderators are not established:
  • the intergroup extension failed a well-powered preregistered replication;
  • the load effect rests on about 23 per cell plus one non-preregistered conceptual study.
  • Do not cite Valdesolo & DeSteno for "hypocrisy is a quick intuitive bias that deliberation corrects" as settled.

Gaps

  • No direct or preregistered replication of the 2008 cognitive-load effect was found.
  • I did not locate or open Du et al. (2019) or Li et al. (2021), which Sun et al. cite as partly inconsistent.

3. Moral licensing meta-analyses and large replications

Takeaway

The early meta-analytic estimate (d = 0.31; Blanken et al. 2015) shrinks substantially after bias correction: - Kuper & Bott 2019: PET-PEESE d = −0.05, 3PSM d = 0.18. - Three well-powered direct replications of Sachdeva et al. (2009) failed.

The 2024–2026 meta-analyses give a narrower verdict: licensing appears reliably only under specific conditions: - when the outcome is a negative behaviour (g = 0.25); - when participants are observed (bias-corrected g = 0.51 observed versus −0.01 unobserved).

This last split matters for the LLM framing, because it points to a reputational rather than intrapsychic mechanism.

Cited Findings

  • Blanken, van de Ven & Zeelenberg (2015). PSPB 41(4), 540–558, DOI 10.1177/0146167215572134.
  • 91 studies, 7,397 participants; "the magnitude of the moral licensing effect is estimated to be a Cohen's d of 0.31."
  • Published studies showed larger effects than unpublished ones, and no other hypothesized moderators were supported.
  • The authors say future studies need much larger samples.
  • Source: Tilburg University record (opened).
  • Blanken, van de Ven, Zeelenberg & Meijers (2014). "Three attempts to replicate the moral licensing effect", Social Psychology 45(3), 232–238, DOI 10.1027/1864-9335/a000189.
  • Studies 1 and 2 used students (N = 105 and N = 150, 95 % power from the original effect). Study 3 used MTurk (N = 940, 95 % power from a self-licensing meta-analysis).
  • None of the three confirmed Sachdeva, Iliev & Medin (2009).
  • The authors concluded there is no strong support yet for the moral self-regulation framework, and that the manipulation is unlikely to induce licensing.
  • Source: Tilburg University record (opened).
  • Kuper & Bott (2019). "Has the evidence for moral licensing been inflated by publication bias?", Meta-Psychology 3, DOI 10.15626/MP.2018.878.
  • Using the larger of two prior meta-analytic datasets, they found PET-PEESE d = −0.05 (p = .64) and 3PSM d = 0.18 (p = .002).
  • They note the first estimate may underestimate the effect and the second may still exaggerate it.
  • Conclusion: "both the evidence for and the size of moral licensing effects has likely been inflated by publication bias." Culture moderated the effect.
  • Source: Meta-Psychology article page (opened).
  • Ferguson, Kaufmann, Brown & de la Piedad Garcia (2024). Psychological Bulletin 150(6), 694–726, DOI 10.1037/bul0000441. Source: OpenAlex abstract (metadata).
  • Meta-Analysis 1 (k = 217, N = 31,242): a prior positive act produced licensing only when the target was a negative behaviour, g = 0.25, 95 % CI [0.16, 0.44]. Foot-in-the-door studies with prosocial requests instead showed consistency, g = −0.44.
  • Meta-Analysis 2 (k = 132, N = 14,443): compensation appeared only for positive targets, g = 0.27.
  • The authors: "These results cannot be readily explained by current theories."
  • Rotella, Jung, Chinn & Barclay. "Observation moderates the moral licensing effect: A meta-analytic test of interpersonal and intrapsychic mechanisms", PSPB 52(9), 2792–2808 (online July 2025; volume year 2026). Article DOI 10.1177/01461672251345512 (from search); preprint DOI 10.31234/osf.io/tmhe9_v2. Source: Northumbria research portal (opened).
  • Preregistered multilevel meta-analysis: 115 experiments, N = 21,770.
  • Licensing g = 0.65 when observed versus g = 0.13 when unobserved.
  • After robust Bayesian bias correction: observed g = 0.51 (BF10 = 9.14); unobserved g = −0.01 (BF10 = 0.11, moderate evidence against).
  • Ambiguity did not moderate the effect. "Evidence for intrapsychic motives is inconclusive."
  • The authors suggest this may explain failed online replications.

Inferences

  • Current verdict. Licensing as a general, private, self-concept-driven effect is doubtful. A conditional, reputational licensing effect has moderate support.
  • For an AI paper, licensing (good act → later bad act) is a weak analogy unless the paper frames it as observation- or audience-dependent.

Gaps

  • I found no Many-Labs-style multi-site registered replication of moral licensing, only the Blanken et al. (2014) three-study set.
  • Simbrunner & Schlegelmilch (2017), a culture-moderated meta-analysis, was seen only as a search-result title. Not opened.

4. Self-concept maintenance, retractions and misconduct cases

Takeaway

The flagship self-concept-maintenance result (Mazar, Amir & Ariely 2008, Ten Commandments) has three strikes against it: - it failed a 25-lab Registered Replication Report (N = 5,786; effect d = −0.04, in the opposite direction); - the paper has carried a JMR Expression of Concern since 2024-09-20; - the most-cited "honesty pledge" paper (Shu et al. 2012) was retracted in 2021 for fabricated data in two studies.

Four Gino papers on dishonesty and morality are retracted. Lammers, Stapel & Galinsky (2010) "Power increases hypocrisy" was cleared and is not retracted.

Cited Findings

  • The Registered Replication Report. Verschuere, Meijer, Jim, Hoogesteyn, Orthey, McCarthy, Skowronski et al. (2018), "Registered Replication Report on Mazar, Amir, and Ariely (2008)", AMPPS 1(3), 299–317, DOI 10.1177/2515245918781032. Source: City St George's open-access PDF (opened; text extracted).
  • The protocol was vetted by Mazar, Ariely and Amir.
  • The original Experiment 1 (N = 229) found 1.45 fewer matrices reported after the Ten Commandments prime (d = 0.48), and its abstract says it "has not been replicated directly."
  • 25 direct replications, total N = 5,786. The primary meta-analysis (19 labs, n = 4,674) found 0.11 more matrices after the moral reminder, 95 % CI [−0.09, 0.31], d = −0.04, CI [−0.12, 0.04], "numerically in the opposite direction."
  • Baseline cheating was tiny: books-cheat minus books-control was 0.15 matrices, 95 % CI [−0.03, 0.34], against 1.16 in the original.
  • The 2024 Expression of Concern on the original. Mazar, Amir & Ariely (2008), J. Marketing Research 45(6), 633–644, DOI 10.1509/jmkr.45.6.633, carries "Expression of Concern: 'The Dishonesty of Honest People: A Theory of Self-Concept Maintenance'", DOI 10.1177/00222437241285882, dated 2024-09-20. Source: Crossref record of the paper, updated-by field and Crossref record of the notice (metadata).
  • The SAGE notice page returned 403, so the notice's stated reasons were not read.
  • Secondary sources (search-only: a Straight Dope forum and a Remy Levin thread relayed on Andrew Gelman's blog) link it to data provenance and the failed RRR. Treat those reasons as unverified.
  • The retraction of Shu, Mazar, Gino, Ariely & Bazerman (2012). PNAS 109, 15197–15200, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1209746109.
  • Retracted 2021-09-13. Notice: "Retraction for Shu et al.", DOI 10.1073/pnas.2115397118, pnas.org notice. Sources: Crossref (metadata); Retraction Watch, 2021-09-14 (opened).
  • Notice wording: "The editors are retracting this article and note that Simonsohn, Simmons, and Nelson … have provided evidence to question the validity of the data in the article." The authors had requested retraction in July/August 2021.
  • Two separate fabrications. Data Colada [109] "Clusterfake" documents hand-moved rows in Study 1 (UNC lab study, N = 101; eight suspicious rows alone give t(6) = 21.92). Study 3, the auto-insurer field experiment, had fabricated data "introduced by someone other than the Study 1 tamperer." Harvard asked PNAS to amend the notice to mention the additional fraud. Source: Data Colada 109 (opened).
  • The 2020 failed replication by the original team. Kristal, Whillans, Bazerman, Gino, Shu, Mazar & Ariely (2020), "Signing at the beginning versus at the end does not decrease dishonesty", PNAS 117, 7103–7107, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1911695117.
  • Five conceptual replications (n = 4,559) and one preregistered direct replication (n = 1,235) found "no effect of signing first on honest reporting." Source: OpenAlex abstract (metadata).
  • Crossref lists a correction dated 2026-06-29 (DOI 10.1073/pnas.2610441123). Its content could not be opened (403).
  • Duke and Ariely. Duke's confidential investigation of the 2012 paper concluded that Ariely "had not knowingly falsified data but should have vetted and preserved it better." In Aug–Sept 2026 Data Colada [138]/[139] alleged tampering in Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002, Psychological Science, procrastination). Wertenbroch requested retraction, and the journal's review is ongoing. That paper is not hypocrisy-related. Source: Poets&Quants, 2026-09-02 (opened; secondary news).
  • The Gino case: retracted papers and dates (all from Crossref metadata, updated-by fields): 1. Shu et al. (2012), PNAS: retracted 2021-09-13. Topic: honesty pledges and dishonesty. 2. Gino & Wiltermuth (2014), "Evil genius? How dishonesty can lead to greater creativity", Psychological Science 25, 973–981: retracted 2023-07-06 (notice DOI 10.1177/09567976231187595). 3. Gino, Kouchaki & Galinsky (2015), "The moral virtue of authenticity: How inauthenticity produces feelings of immorality and impurity", Psychological Science 26, 983–996: retracted 2023-07-06 (notice DOI 10.1177/09567976231187596). 4. Gino, Kouchaki & Casciaro (2020), "Why connect? Moral consequences of networking with a promotion or prevention focus", JPSP 119(6), 1221–1238: retracted 2023-08-17 online. Notice "Retraction of Gino et al. (2020)", JPSP 125, p. 648, DOI 10.1037/pspa0000351. A search summary of the PubMed notice says the retraction was requested by HBS's Research Integrity Officer over Study 3a, and that co-authors Kouchaki and Casciaro were not investigated and agreed.
  • Gino: other papers and later events.
  • "The counterfeit self" (Gino, Norton & Ariely 2010, Psychological Science 21, 712–720, DOI 10.1177/0956797610366545) shows no retraction or EoC in Crossref (metadata). This does not certify its data.
  • Harvard found misconduct in 2023 and revoked Gino's tenure in May 2025 (Harvard Crimson, 2025-05-27, as cited by Retraction Record; opened, secondary aggregator).
  • Gino's defamation claims against Harvard and all claims against the Data Colada authors were dismissed in September 2024. Her contract and discrimination claims and Harvard's counterclaim were pending as of September 2026, with trial set for 7 December. Sources: Retraction Record (opened); dismissal also reported by Science (search-only).
  • Stapel: "Power increases hypocrisy". Lammers, Stapel & Galinsky (2010), Psychological Science 21, 737–744, DOI 10.1177/0956797610368810.
  • Retraction Watch (2012-06-29) lists it among Psychological Science papers co-authored by Stapel that the Levelt Committee cleared; the investigation "found no evidence of fraudulent data or practices." Source: Retraction Watch (opened).
  • Crossref shows no retraction, EoC or correction (Crossref, metadata).

Inferences

  • Self-concept-maintenance theory is not refuted by these events, but its two flagship demonstrations are gone or compromised:
  • moral reminders reduce cheating: null at scale, plus an EoC;
  • signing first reduces dishonesty: retracted, plus a null preregistered replication.
  • Citing Mazar et al. (2008) or Shu et al. (2012) as evidence is now inadvisable. If cited at all, cite them with the RRR, the EoC and the retraction.
  • "Power increases hypocrisy" is formally clean, but it carries a Stapel co-authorship. The search found no independent direct replication. Cite it with care.
  • None of the four retracted Gino papers is a moral-hypocrisy paper in Batson's sense. They are adjacent (dishonesty, moral self-perception, moral impurity), so the Batson paradigm itself is not implicated.

Gaps

  • The text of the JMR Expression of Concern (2024) and the PNAS 2026 correction to Kristal et al. could not be opened (403).
  • I verified only Data Colada [109]. The mapping of [110]–[112] to specific papers was not opened.
  • I did not check whether other Gino papers on hypocrisy or ethics carry EoCs, beyond "The counterfeit self".

5. Moral wiggle room and willful ignorance meta-analysis

Takeaway

Vu et al. (2023, Psychological Bulletin) pooled 56 treatment effects (6,531 participants, 33,603 decisions): - about 40 % of participants avoid easily obtainable information about how their choices affect others; - this costs about 15.6 percentage points of altruistic behaviour; - the evidence fits excuse-seeking to protect self-image.

This is the most robust, quantitatively pooled neighbour of moral hypocrisy, though largely from European and US samples.

Cited Findings

  • The meta-analysis. Vu, L., Soraperra, I., Leib, M., van der Weele, J., & Shalvi, S. (2023). "Ignorance by choice: A meta-analytic review of the underlying motives of willful ignorance and its consequences." Psychological Bulletin, 149(9–10), 611–635. DOI 10.1037/bul0000398. Source: Tilburg University record (opened).
  • 33,603 decisions, 6,531 participants, 56 treatment effects, all variants of one paradigm.
  • About 40 % avoided information; avoidance went with a 15.6-pp drop in altruism.
  • The authors interpret this as excuse-seeking aimed at maintaining a positive self-image.
  • Search-only details.
  • The exact figure is 39.8 %.
  • People who chose to learn the consequences were about 7 pp more generous than those given the information by default.
  • Inattention and reputation could not be ruled out.
  • Samples were overwhelmingly European and US.
  • Treat these as unverified.
  • The original paradigm. Dana, Weber & Kuang, "Exploiting moral wiggle room: Experiments demonstrating an illusory preference for fairness", Economic Theory, DOI 10.1007/s00199-006-0153-z (online 2006, issue 2007; 1,452 OpenAlex citations). Source: OpenAlex (metadata). The often-quoted "44 % chose to remain ignorant" figure was search-only.

Inferences

  • Relevance to the LLM paper. Wiggle room offers a more replicable empirical anchor than the Batson coin flip for "fairness preference that evaporates when excuses are available". Its mechanism (excuse-seeking) maps onto "write the faithful analysis, then act otherwise when the framing gives cover."

Gaps

  • I did not open the full Vu et al. text for moderator results, publication-bias tests, or the share of studies run in economics labs.

6. Cheating paradigm meta-analyses

Takeaway

Both large meta-analyses find widespread but partial dishonesty, with substantial publication bias: - Gerlach et al. 2019: 565 experiments, N = 44,050; about 38–42 % lie. - Abeler et al. 2019: people forgo about three-quarters of the potential gains from lying. They are motivated both by a preference for being honest and by a preference for being seen as honest.

Cited Findings

  • Gerlach, Teodorescu & Hertwig (2019). "The truth about lies: A meta-analysis on dishonest behavior", Psychological Bulletin 145(1), 1–44, DOI 10.1037/bul0000174.
  • Four paradigms: sender-receiver games, die roll, coin flip, matrix. 565 experiments, N = 44,050 choices.
  • Dishonesty depends on reward magnitude, externalities, gender and age.
  • Lab studies show more dishonesty than field studies, and deception in experiments goes with less dishonesty.
  • Die-roll liars lie "to a considerably greater degree" than matrix liars at a comparable rate.
  • "substantial evidence for publication bias in almost all measures of dishonest behavior."
  • Source: OpenAlex abstract (metadata).
  • The MPIB press release (2019-01-08) adds: 42 % of men and 38 % of women lied; the lying probability fell 0.28 pp per year of age, from about 47 % at 20 to about 35 % at 60. Source: MPIB press release (opened).
  • Abeler, Nosenzo & Raymond (2019). "Preferences for truth-telling", Econometrica 87(4), 1115–1153, DOI 10.3982/ECTA14673.
  • 90 experimental studies in economics, psychology and sociology: "people lie surprisingly little."
  • "a preference for being seen as honest and a preference for being honest are the main motivations for truth-telling."
  • Source: LISER record (opened).
  • The IZA DP 10188 (2016) working-paper version covers 72 studies, more than 32,000 subjects and 43 countries: "subjects forgo on average about three-quarters of the potential gains from lying." Source: IZA PDF (opened; text extracted).
  • The published version's "44,000+ subjects, 47 countries" was search-only.

Inferences

  • Both meta-analyses support partial dishonesty: most people shade rather than maximize. Abeler et al.'s "being seen as honest" term echoes impression-management accounts of hypocrisy.
  • The publication bias Gerlach et al. found means individual effect sizes in this literature should be treated as upper bounds.

Gaps

  • I did not extract paradigm-specific pooled rates from the Gerlach et al. full text (e.g., the share of liars in coin-flip tasks specifically).

7. Current expert framing of hypocrisy

Takeaway

Two research programmes now coexist: - Batson's motive construct: wanting to appear moral while avoiding the cost. - A larger, more active perception literature (Effron; Jordan; Monin): there "hypocrisy" is a judgment observers make about word–deed inconsistency.

In the perception literature, hypocrisy is explicitly distinguished from mere inconsistency. Condemnation is explained by false signalling, and it is conditional ("can—but do not always—trigger a hypocrisy penalty"). The open debates are three: - self-deception versus impression management; - whether the coin-flip behaviour reflects a hypocritical motive at all (Lin & Miller); - inconsistency versus hypocrisy.

Cited Findings

  • Effron, O'Connor, Leroy & Lucas (2018). "From inconsistency to hypocrisy: When does 'saying one thing but doing another' invite condemnation?", Research in Organizational Behavior 38, 61–75, DOI 10.1016/j.riob.2018.10.003. Source: OpenAlex (metadata: title and authors; 133 citations).
  • The authors recast hypocrisy from "failing to practise what you preach" into a "subjective interpretation" of why words and deeds diverge, so "not every leader who says one thing and does another will be seen as a hypocrite." Source: London Business School Think article (opened).
  • The full text was not opened (CORE returned 403).
  • Jordan, Sommers, Bloom & Rand (2017). "Why do we hate hypocrites? Evidence for a theory of false signaling", Psychological Science 28(3), 356–368, DOI 10.1177/0956797616685771. Five studies:
  • condemnation signals virtue more strongly than direct claims;
  • hypocrites are judged more harshly than people who directly lie about their morality;
  • "honest" hypocrites who admit the transgression are not viewed negatively.
  • Source: APS journal page (opened).
  • Jordan & Sommers (2022). "When does moral engagement risk triggering a hypocrite penalty?", Current Opinion in Psychology, DOI 10.1016/j.copsyc.2022.101404. Source: OpenAlex (metadata: title and authors). The review's conclusion that hypocrites "can—but do not always—trigger a 'hypocrisy penalty'" was search-only.
  • Batson (2016) and its reception. What's Wrong with Morality? A Social-Psychological Perspective (OUP). Carbonell's review in Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews (2016-04-10) does four things:
  • it reports Batson's definition of hypocrisy as a "motivation to appear moral while, if possible, avoiding the cost of actually being moral" (p. 94);
  • it calls the lab studies "sterile and artificial" and "at best a pale sliver" of moral life;
  • it notes that data are given only in summary;
  • it says "I am just not sure we understand the precise nature of the doom yet."
  • Source: NDPR review (opened).
  • The self-deception versus impression-management debate. Two primary sources take opposite sides:
  • Batson et al. (1999) frame hypocrisy as self-deception, specifically avoiding behaviour–standard comparison (OpenAlex).
  • Lönnqvist et al. (2014) argue for impression management (EconPapers).
  • Dong et al. (2019) propose a self-enhancement account: people want to appear better than others (PLoS ONE).
  • The dynamic reinterpretation. Lin & Miller (2021) argue that what looked like planned hypocrisy may be revision of preferences in the moment (IDEAS).
  • Monin & Merritt (2012). Their chapter, "Moral hypocrisy, moral inconsistency, and the struggle for moral integrity", is cited for the claim that hypocrisy and inconsistency are distinct constructs (search-only; the chapter itself was not located or opened).

Inferences

  • For the LLM paper, the safest framing is behavioural and perceiver-relative. The model's analysis and its action exhibit word–deed misalignment. An observer could read that as hypocrisy in Batson's sense, but the paper does not claim a motive to appear moral.
  • The Effron line explicitly separates "inconsistency" from "hypocrisy". Calling the LLM behaviour "hypocrisy" invites the objection that it is mere inconsistency, or that there was a changed preference (Lin & Miller) or overpowered integrity (Batson & Thompson 2001).
  • "Overpowered integrity" — an intention to be moral abandoned when costs become salient — may fit the "faithful analysis, then compromising action" pattern better than hypocrisy proper.
  • Hypocrisy is treated today as a family: a motive (Batson), a judgment (Effron/Jordan), and a set of behavioural signatures (self-serving procedures, self-other asymmetry). It is not one robust effect with one mechanism.

Gaps

  • No 2023–2026 integrative review in Annual Review of Psychology or Perspectives on Psychological Science specifically on moral hypocrisy was found. A ResearchGate "Moral Hypocrisy: The Antecedents and Outcomes" systematic review exists, but its year and venue are unverified.
  • I did not locate a Graham et al. "state of the field" treatment of hypocrisy.
  • The full texts of Effron et al. (2018) and Jordan & Sommers (2022) were not opened.

8. Cross-cultural and individual-difference findings

Takeaway

Condemnation of hypocrisy is culturally variable: it is weaker in interdependent cultures (Effron et al. 2018, JESP; three experiments plus a 46-nation survey). Licensing effects are culture-moderated (Kuper & Bott 2019). On the individual-difference side: - values predict integrity versus hypocrisy (Universalism versus Conformity; Lönnqvist et al.); - dishonesty shows small gender and age effects (Gerlach et al.).

The core paradigms rest heavily on Western student and online samples.

Cited Findings

  • Culture and condemnation. Effron, Markus, Jackman, Muramoto & Muluk (2018), "Hypocrisy and culture: Failing to practice what you preach receives harsher interpersonal reactions in independent (vs. interdependent) cultures", JESP 76, 371–384, DOI 10.1016/j.jesp.2017.12.009. Source: LBS Research Online (opened).
  • Studies 1–3: the condemnation boost for contradicted values was weaker in Indonesia, India and Japan than in the USA.
  • Study 4: employees from 46 nations; perceived manager misalignment reduced trust less where cultures emphasize interdependence.
  • Proposed mechanism: in interdependent cultures, preaching is read as other-oriented.
  • Culture and licensing. Culture moderated moral licensing in Kuper & Bott (2019) (Meta-Psychology).
  • Values. Universalism values predicted moral integrity and Conformity values predicted hypocrisy (Lönnqvist et al.; EconPapers).
  • Age and gender in dishonesty. Small effects: 42 % of men versus 38 % of women lied, and the lying probability fell 0.28 pp per year of age (MPIB).
  • China. Batson-style task assignment in a Chinese sample showed 48 % hypocrites in the neutral control, rising to about 80 % under negative mood (Sun et al. 2025; PMC).
  • Children. A Wilfrid Laurier thesis on the development of hypocrisy in children reports that 62.2 % of children who flipped reported winning (search-only; WLU thesis, not opened).

Inferences

  • Whether observers (including human readers of an AI paper) label an inconsistency as hypocrisy depends on culture. This supports the perceiver-relative framing in §7.

Gaps

  • No cross-cultural direct replication of the Batson coin-flip behaviour was found. The Chinese adaptation used a rigged draw rather than a coin.
  • The willful-ignorance and cheating meta-analyses are dominated by WEIRD samples (Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic). Vu et al.'s statement to this effect was search-only.
