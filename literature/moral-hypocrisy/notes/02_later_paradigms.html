Experimental paradigms that extended or paralleled Batson's moral-hypocrisy research (c. 2000–2026)

Provenance key used throughout: [PRIMARY-FULL] = I opened the full text (or the authors' working-paper full text) of the paper itself; [PRIMARY-ABSTRACT] = I opened the publisher/Crossref/repository abstract only; [SECONDARY] = the claim comes from a press release, review, replication report or other paper describing the study. Bibliographic data (volume, pages, DOI) were checked against Crossref records (api.crossref.org) unless noted otherwise. Batson's own studies and the general replication-crisis literature are deliberately left out (other researchers cover them); replication facts appear only where they matter for a given paradigm.

Integrity flags at a glance (checked 2026-11-04): - Lammers, Stapel & Galinsky (2010) — not retracted; the Levelt Committee cleared it (June 2012 notice). Crossref shows no correction or retraction record. - Mazar, Amir & Ariely (2008, JMR) — Expression of Concern, Journal of Marketing Research 61:1183, dated 20 Sept 2024 (DOI 10.1177/00222437241285882). Q1. Valdesolo & DeSteno (2007, 2008): judging one's own versus another's transgression; the group boundary; cognitive load removes hypocrisy

Takeaway

Valdesolo & DeSteno turned Batson's task-assignment paradigm into a judgment paradigm. Participants either commit the unfair assignment themselves or watch someone else commit it, then rate how fair it was. People rate their own transgression as fairer than an identical one by another person; in 2007 that leniency also covers in-group members. Their 2008 paper shows the self-leniency disappears under cognitive load, and they conclude that hypocrisy comes from effortful, deliberative justification layered on top of an intuitive aversion to unfairness that people apply to everyone alike. The 2007 group effect failed a large preregistered replication in 2024.

Cited Findings

Valdesolo, P., & DeSteno, D. (2007). Moral hypocrisy: Social groups and the flexibility of virtue. Psychological Science, 18(8), 689–690. DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01961.x [Crossref bibliographic data; design and results from SECONDARY press release; full text paywalled and not opened] - Design: participants allocated an easy, enjoyable task and a hard, tedious task between themselves and an anonymous second party. They could use a random procedure described as "guaranteeing fairness" or assign the tasks as they wished. Others rated the fairness of an identical assignment made by a second party. Group membership was manipulated with a trivial, fictitious attribute: the second party was described as "similar", "not similar", or not described at all (minimal groups). — Northeastern University press release, 8 Aug 2007 - About 88% of participants gave themselves the easy task without using the random procedure. They judged their own unfair act "much more mildly" than the same act by unknown others. When the transgressor was "similar" (in-group), participants excused the act and judged it "no more unfair" than their own. The release gives no N and no rating means. — Northeastern press release - Valdesolo's quote: "The mind is inherently social, and left unchecked, it will bias judgment in the service of ourselves." — Northeastern press release - Replication status: Robertson, Akles & Van Bavel (2024), Psychological Science 35(7), 798–813, DOI 10.1177/09567976241246552, ran a direct replication with minimal groups (N = 610, nationally representative US Prolific sample) and a conceptual replication with political groups (N = 606, 50% Democrats and 50% Republicans). The abstract reads: "the results did not replicate the original findings". It did find in-group favoritism in the minimal-groups study and out-group derogation in the political study, and the authors call the overall evidence for intergroup moral hypocrisy "mixed". — Crossref abstract, PRIMARY-ABSTRACT

Valdesolo, P., & DeSteno, D. (2008). The duality of virtue: Deconstructing the moral hypocrite. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 44(5), 1334–1338. DOI 10.1016/j.jesp.2008.03.010 [Crossref bibliographic data; design and results from SECONDARY sources; the author-hosted PDF link on Valdesolo's CV returned 404] - Design: participants could assign themselves and another person a fun or an onerous task, either at random or by their own choice. A second group watched another person give themselves the better task. Everyone then rated how fair the act was. Under cognitive load, participants held a random digit string in memory while judging. — Northeastern University press release (2008). Secondary reporting gives the load as a string of seven digits. — Freakonomics guest post by the authors' circle, 28 Jul 2008, SECONDARY - Result: without load, people rated their own transgression significantly fairer than the same act by another. Under load the bias vanished: participants "judged their own transgressions to be as unfair as the same behavior enacted by another." — Northeastern press release (2008) - Authors' interpretation: hypocrisy "emerges from deliberative processes" involving "volitionally-guided justifications". Underneath is a basic negative reaction to fairness violations, whether committed by oneself or by others, which rationalization then softens. This is a dual-process account. — Northeastern press release (2008) - Later citing summary: "Valdesolo and DeSteno (2008) saw the self-serving bias that is typically observed in the hypocrisy literature disappear when imposing their subjects to high cognitive load; these subjects judged a moral transgression performed by themselves as unfair… indicating they had no cognitive capacity to make self-serving justifications." — van 't Veer, Stel & van Beest (2014), Judgment and Decision Making 9(3), 199–206, SECONDARY - A related finding: van 't Veer et al. (2014) found "considerable lying when cognitive capacity was not limited" and "no detectable lying when cognitive capacity was limited". This fits the view that self-serving dishonesty needs cognitive resources. — van 't Veer et al. 2014 - Contrary evidence on automaticity: Shalvi, Eldar & Bereby-Meyer (2012), Psychological Science 23(10), 1264–1270, DOI 10.1177/0956797612443835, found more die-roll lying under time pressure, and read self-serving lying as the automatic tendency. Two preregistered direct replications in 2020 (N = 428 and N = 297) found that time pressure did not increase cheating. — search-surfaced summary of Shalvi et al. 2012 and the 2020 replications, SECONDARY; van 't Veer et al. discuss the tension explicitly: "this dual-system framework has left researchers with evidence pointing in opposite directions." — van 't Veer et al. 2014 - Emotion extension: Polman & Ruttan (2012), PSPB 38(1), 129–139, DOI 10.1177/0146167211422365. Using the self-versus-other judgment design with moral dilemmas (Study 1) and real cancer-research donations (Study 2), they found that "anger increased moral hypocrisy, guilt eliminated moral hypocrisy, and envy reversed moral hypocrisy." — Crossref abstract, PRIMARY-ABSTRACT

Inferences

  • The 2008 paper is the standard citation for the claim that self-serving leniency is a controlled-process add-on: the intuitive verdict ("this is unfair") is the same for self and other, and the self-favoring gap appears only once deliberation is free to rationalize. For the AI paper, the parallel is that a model's analysis can correctly identify the priority (the unbiased verdict) while the action step adds a justification that softens it. This is an analogy only; nothing in this literature shows LLMs have a dual-process architecture.
  • Cite the 2007 in-group finding with its failed 2024 replication. Do not use it as an established effect.

Gaps

  • I could not open either Valdesolo & DeSteno full text. The 2008 N, rating scale, cell means and test statistics are unverified; the 2007 N and means are unverified. Get the PDFs through a library before quoting numbers.

Q2. Lammers, Stapel & Galinsky (2010): power and hypocrisy, and the paper's status after the Stapel fraud

Takeaway

In five experiments, power made people stricter about others' transgressions and more lenient about their own, and the powerful also cheated more on a private dice task. The effect reversed ("hypercrisy") when power was illegitimate and among low-power participants. Although Stapel is a coauthor, the paper is not retracted: the Levelt Committee explicitly cleared it, and Crossref shows no retraction or correction.

Cited Findings

Lammers, J., Stapel, D. A., & Galinsky, A. D. (2010). Power increases hypocrisy: Moralizing in reasoning, immorality in behavior. Psychological Science, 21(5), 737–744. DOI 10.1177/0956797610368810 [PRIMARY-ABSTRACT via Crossref; method details SECONDARY] - Abstract: "In five studies, we explored whether power increases moral hypocrisy (i.e., imposing strict moral standards on other people but practicing less strict moral behavior oneself). In Experiment 1, compared with the powerless, the powerful condemned other people's cheating more, but also cheated more themselves. In Experiments 2 through 4, the powerful were more strict in judging other people's moral transgressions than in judging their own transgressions. A final study found that the effect of power on moral hypocrisy depends on the legitimacy of the power: When power was illegitimate, the moral-hypocrisy effect was reversed… This pattern, which might be dubbed hypercrisy, was also found among low-power participants in Experiments 3 and 4." — Crossref record - Methods (secondary): power was manipulated by role (e.g., prime minister versus civil servant). Cheating was measured with a dice game played alone in a cubicle, where high-power participants reported winning more lottery tickets. The moral dilemmas covered breaking traffic rules, declaring taxes and keeping a stolen bike. The page reports no statistics. — Kellogg School news release, 2009, SECONDARY - Status: Retraction Watch (Adam Marcus, 29 June 2012) reported that Psychological Science retracted two Ruys & Stapel (2008) emotion papers. The same notice lists Lammers, Stapel & Galinsky (2010) among the Stapel-coauthored articles that "have been cleared by the Levelt Committee", whose investigation "found no evidence of fraudulent data or practices". Also cleared: Lammers, Stoker, Jordan, Pollmann & Stapel (2011) and Lammers, Stoker & Stapel (2009). — Retraction Watch, 29 Jun 2012 - In November 2026, the Crossref record for DOI 10.1177/0956797610368810 shows no "updated-by" entry (no retraction, correction or expression of concern). By contrast, Crossref does show such an entry for Mazar et al. 2008 (see Q5). — Crossref API record

Inferences

  • Safe description: "Lammers, Stapel & Galinsky (2010; cleared by the Levelt Committee in 2012 and not retracted)". The footnote is worth including because reviewers will notice Stapel's name.

Gaps

  • I did not open the full text, so per-study Ns, effect sizes, and whether power in Experiment 1 was manipulated by recall or by role are unverified. The Kellogg release says role.
  • I found no independent replication of the power–hypocrisy effect within this budget.

Q3. Lönnqvist, Irlenbusch & Walkowitz (2014); Rustichini & Villeval (2014); hypocrisy and Schwartz values; economics variants

Takeaway

Economists moved Batson's coin paradigm into dictator games with real stakes and asked whether hypocrisy is self-deception or impression management. Lönnqvist et al. favor impression management: people accept a fair coin only when they can fudge it, and hypocrisy tracks Schwartz Conformity, while Universalism predicts integrity. Rustichini & Villeval show the reverse direction of fit: people revise their stated fairness principles to match the selfish choices they just made, and stated fairness norms already bend toward whoever holds bargaining power, "a compromise with power".

Cited Findings

Lönnqvist, J.-E., Irlenbusch, B., & Walkowitz, G. (2014). Moral hypocrisy: Impression management or self-deception? Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 55, 53–62. DOI 10.1016/j.jesp.2014.06.004 [PRIMARY-ABSTRACT via the LSE repository; Crossref gives pp. 53–62] - Three studies, N = 256, in dictator-game settings. Moral hypocrisy appeared within participants (saying one thing and doing another; Study 1) and between participants (behaving one way when the choice is inconsequential and another when it affects payoffs; Study 2). — LSE Research Online record; search summary of the abstract - Study 3: participants "were willing to let an ostensibly fair coin determine payoffs only if they could fudge the results of the coin flip, suggesting that hypocrites do not deceive themselves." — abstract via LSE/KUPS - Schwartz values link: hypocrisy tracked Conformity values (Studies 1–2), "indicative of a desire to appear moral in the eyes of others but not indicative of self-deception". Universalism values predicted moral integrity (Studies 1 and 3). — abstract via LSE/KUPS - A 2013 conference version (Walkowitz, Lönnqvist & Irlenbusch) had four studies (N = 320). Its fourth study reported that hypocrisy "can be reduced by increasing the collective awareness about its prevalence". It is unclear whether that study made it into the 2014 article. — EconPapers/VfS 2013 record, SECONDARY

Rustichini, A., & Villeval, M. C. (2014). Moral hypocrisy, power and social preferences. Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 107(A), 10–24. DOI 10.1016/j.jebo.2014.08.002 [PRIMARY-FULL of the working paper, IZA DP 6590, May 2012; the JEBO version was not opened, so treat numbers as WP numbers] - Design: in session 1, participants state, with sliders, the ranges of allocations they consider fair and unfair in a Dictator, an Ultimatum and a Trust game scenario, plus hypothetical choices. About one week later (session 2) they play the same games for money, one-shot with random re-matching, and then state their fairness principles again. Initial principles were deliberately not shown to them in session 2. — IZA DP 6590 full text - Sample: 5 sets of 2 sessions at the GATE laboratory (Lyon); 83 participants completed both sessions, 52% female. — IZA DP 6590 - Abstract findings: "norms that appear abstract and universal take into account the bargaining power of the two sides. The strong side bends the norm in its favor and the weak side agrees: Stated fairness is a compromise with power. Second, in most situations, individuals adjust the range of fair shares after playing the game for real money… Third, the discrepancy between hypothetical and real behavior is larger in games where real choices have no strategic consequence (Dictator game and second mover in Trust game) than in those where they do (Ultimatum game). Finally the adjustment of principles to actions is mainly the fact of individuals who behave more selfishly and who have a stronger bargaining power." — IZA DP 6590 - Interpretation: the hypocrisy, measured as the gap between statements and actions followed by adjustment of principles to actions, "appears produced by the attempt, not necessarily conscious, to strike a balance between self-image and immediate convenience." — IZA DP 6590. The JEBO abstract describes this as "possibly self-deceptive adjustment of judgments to actions… produced by the attempt to strike a compromise between self-image and payoffs." — EconPapers JEBO record via search summary

Other economics variants of the coin/dictator design (see Q4 for moral wiggle room proper) - Delegation: Hamman, Loewenstein & Weber (2010), "Self-interest through delegation", AER 100(4), 1826–1846, DOI 10.1257/aer.100.4.1826. When allocations are made through agents, "principals keep more and recipients receive less". The working paper reports that in one condition less than 1% of the endowment was shared on average, and that accountability becomes "vertically diffused with no individual taking responsibility." — IZA conference WP 2007, SECONDARY/WP; EconPapers record - Risk as an excuse: Exley (2016), "Excusing selfishness in charitable giving: The role of risk", Review of Economic Studies 83(2), 587–628, DOI 10.1093/restud/rdv051. With no self-versus-charity tradeoff, participants respond similarly to risk to themselves and risk to the charity. With a tradeoff, they become more averse to charity risk and less averse to self risk, which biases choices toward themselves. Exley reads this as excuse-driven. — abstract via search summary of IDEAS/REStud

Inferences

  • Lönnqvist et al. give the paper a link to Schwartz values: hypocrisy goes with Conformity (concern for appearing moral), integrity with Universalism. For the repo's Schwartz lane this is the one study I found that ties moral hypocrisy directly to the value-priority profile.
  • Rustichini & Villeval's "stated fairness is a compromise" is close to the AI finding. After acting, people do not abandon the principle; they move the boundary of "fair" to include what they did. One check for LLM transcripts is whether post-action analysis re-describes the middle option as consistent with the stated priority.

Gaps

  • I did not open the full texts of Lönnqvist et al. or the published JEBO version, so effect sizes and the exact coin/fudging rates are not reported here.
  • Within budget I found no study other than Lönnqvist et al. linking moral hypocrisy to Big Five personality.

Q4. Moral wiggle room: Dana, Weber & Kuang (2007) and successors

Takeaway

In a binary dictator game, 74% choose the fair (5,5) option when consequences are transparent. When transparency is removed (hidden payoffs one free click away, a second dictator, or a random computer "cutoff"), fair outcomes fall to about 35%, roughly half the baseline, while the dictator could still have ensured fairness in every treatment. Dana et al. conclude that much giving reflects a dislike of appearing unfair, to oneself or to others. A 2023 meta-analysis of 56 treatment effects finds that about 40% of people avoid easily available information about consequences, and that avoidance lowers altruistic choices by 15.6 percentage points.

Cited Findings

Dana, J., Weber, R. A., & Kuang, J. X. (2007). Exploiting moral wiggle room: Experiments demonstrating an illusory preference for fairness. Economic Theory, 33(1), 67–80. DOI 10.1007/s00199-006-0153-z (online 2006) [PRIMARY-FULL, author-hosted PDF] - Abstract: treatments in which subjects "are able to leave the relationship between their actions and resulting outcomes uncertain, either to themselves or to another subject… giving subjects the moral 'wiggle room' to behave self-interestedly. We find significantly less generous behavior… We conclude that many subjects behave fairly in the baseline case mainly because they intrinsically dislike appearing unfair, either to themselves or others." — DWK 2007 PDF - 190 subjects in total: baseline (38 subjects, 19 dictators), hidden information (64, 32 dictators), multiple dictator (30, 20 strategic players), plausible deniability (58, 29 dictators). — DWK PDF - Baseline: A = ($6 self, $1 other); B = ($5, $5). 14 of 19 dictators (74%) chose B; all 19 receivers hypothetically chose B. — DWK PDF - Hidden information: the dictator's own payoffs were the same ($6 for A, $5 for B), but the receiver's payoffs were either the baseline ones or "flipped" (A gives the receiver $5), determined by a coin flip. The true payoffs could be revealed costlessly by clicking a button, privately. Only 18 of 32 (56%) revealed. When true payoffs equalled the baseline, 10/16 (63%) chose A, against 26% in the baseline (χ²(1) = 4.64, p = .03). Among those who did not reveal (baseline-payoff matrix), 8/8 (100%) chose A; among those who revealed, 2/8 (25%) chose A. Only 15/32 (47%) revealed and chose the other-regarding option, against 74% in the baseline (χ²(1) = 3.49, p = .06). — DWK PDF, Tables 1–2 - Multiple dictator: two dictators. The unfair ($6, $6, $1) outcome required both to choose A, so either one could impose ($5, $5, $5). Only 35% chose B, against 74% in the baseline (χ²(1) = 5.87, p = .02). All ten passive receivers correctly predicted that A would be most common. — DWK PDF - Plausible deniability: a random cutoff 0–10 s into the choice window, after which the computer picked A or B with equal probability. Only the dictator learned whether a cutoff had occurred. 7/29 (24%) let themselves be cut off (average cutoff about 4.3 s; in a pretest nobody needed more than 2 s). Of those not cut off, 12/22 (55%) chose A. Only 10/29 (34%) of choices were consistent with wanting the fair outcome. Footnote 8: allowing oneself to be cut off "implies preferring a mixture of two outcomes over each one separately, which is inconsistent with a theory of rational choice with utilities defined only over outcomes." — DWK PDF, Table 4 and fn. 8 - Summary regularity: fair outcomes were baseline 74%, hidden information (baseline payoffs) 38%, multiple dictator 35%, plausible deniability 34%. "In all three treatments that relax transparency, the proportion of subjects implementing the fair outcome… is roughly 35%, or half of what it is in the baseline." The authors also stress that "the proportion of dictators who choose—with certainty—to implement the unfair outcome is never greater than one-half" (24% baseline, 13% hidden information, 41% plausible deniability). — DWK PDF, Table 5 and fn. 16 - Suggested mechanism: once transparency is gone, "the norms most consistent with self-interest will often exert stronger influences", for example a "mind your own business" norm justifying not looking. — DWK PDF - A secondary account (Spiekermann 2013) gives the hidden-information numbers as "44% … choose not to reveal… Of those, 86% choose option A". Those are pooled figures; quote the primary Table 1–2 numbers above instead. — Spiekermann, "Four Types of Moral Wriggle Room" (2013), SECONDARY

Larson, T., & Capra, C. M. (2009). Exploiting moral wiggle room: Illusory preference for fairness? A comment. Judgment and Decision Making, 4(6), 467–474. DOI 10.1017/S1930297500004009 [PRIMARY-ABSTRACT via Crossref; the JDM full-text page returned 503] - "By implementing a double-blind experiment together with a design that controls for alternative explanations for the observed behavior, we confirmed DWK's findings." — Crossref abstract. DWK fn. 9 also notes an earlier replication using double-blind anonymity and forcing dictators to take an active step to stay ignorant (Larson 2005), and a within-subjects version with varying state probabilities (Munyan 2005). — DWK PDF

Vu, L., Soraperra, I., Leib, M., van der Weele, J., & Shalvi, S. (2023). Ignorance by choice: A meta-analytic review of the underlying motives of willful ignorance and its consequences. Psychological Bulletin, 149(9–10), 611–635. DOI 10.1037/bul0000398 [PRIMARY-ABSTRACT via search summary of the Tilburg/APA records; Crossref-verified citation] - 33,603 decisions by 6,531 participants in 56 treatment effects (press coverage says 22 studies), all variations of the DWK willful-ignorance paradigm. About 40% of participants avoid easily available information about consequences for others, which leads to a 15.6 percentage-point decrease in altruistic behavior compared with when the information is provided. The results fit excuse-seeking to maintain a positive self-image; much altruism reflects a desire to behave as others expect. All studies were run in US or Western European labs or online. — Tilburg University record; APA press release, Oct 2023 - Not every setting shows strategic exploitation. A 2022 market-setting study found more selfishness with hidden information but "no evidence of a strategic exploitation of moral wiggle room", and van der Weele et al. (2014) found none in trust and moonlighting games. — FU Berlin repository, 2022 JBEE paper, SECONDARY; Innsbruck WP 2019-03, SECONDARY

Inferences

  • The plausible-deniability cutoff is the closest human analogue to a "middle option". About a quarter of dictators let a coin decide, choosing a 50/50 lottery over both the fair and the selfish outcome, which DWK note is inconsistent with outcome-based utility. A middle option that splits the difference offers the same deniability: the agent can say it did not pick the bad outcome.
  • DWK's point that certain, attributable unfairness never exceeded one-half, while unfairness under uncertainty was common, fits the AI finding that models seldom pick the "obviously bad" option but often pick the compromise.

Gaps

  • Larson & Capra's numbers were not retrieved (503). The Vu et al. full text was not opened, so the moderator analyses are not reported.

Q5. Self-concept maintenance and justified dishonesty: Mazar, Amir & Ariely (2008); Shalvi et al. (2011, 2015); Fischbacher & Föllmi-Heusi (2013)

Takeaway

This literature shows that people who value honesty cheat a little, not maximally. Mazar et al. found that only 0.6% (5 of 791) of participants who could cheat did so maximally. In the die-roll paradigm, people forgo about three-quarters of the possible gains from lying, and a distinct group of "partial liars" reports a high-but-not-top number. Justifications such as seeing a desired counterfactual roll, or reporting in tokens instead of money, increase how much people cheat. Integrity flag: Mazar et al. (2008) carries a 2024 Expression of Concern, and its Ten Commandments effect failed a 25-lab Registered Replication Report.

Cited Findings

Mazar, N., Amir, O., & Ariely, D. (2008). The dishonesty of honest people: A theory of self-concept maintenance. Journal of Marketing Research, 45(6), 633–644. DOI 10.1509/jmkr.45.6.633 [PRIMARY-FULL, Duke-hosted PDF] - Abstract: "people behave dishonestly enough to profit but honestly enough to delude themselves of their own integrity… Two mechanisms allow for such self-concept maintenance: inattention to moral standards and categorization malleability. Six experiments…" Hypothesis Ext&Int-H3: "Given the opportunity to be dishonest, people are dishonest up to a certain level that does not force them to update their self-concept." — Mazar et al. PDF - Experiment 1: a 2 (recall 10 high-school books versus the Ten Commandments) × 2 (control versus "recycle", where participants tore out and kept the test sheet, allowing cheating) design. The task was 20 matrices in 4 minutes, with two randomly chosen participants earning $10 per reported solution. Controls solved 3.1 in both reminder conditions; books/recycle reported 4.2; Ten Commandments/recycle reported 2.8 (F(1, 225) = 5.24, p = .023). "On average, participants cheated only 6.7% of the possible magnitude." Predictors (n = 75) expected much more cheating (9.5). — Mazar et al. PDF - Experiment 2 (honor code): N = 207. Recycle (cheat) M = 5.5 against control M = 3.3. Cheating reached "only 13.5% of the possible average magnitude". Signing an honor-code statement brought performance down to 3.0, eliminating cheating, even at institutions without an honor code. — Mazar et al. PDF - Token / categorization-malleability experiment: recycle M = 6.2 against control M = 3.5 (cheating at 16.5% of maximum). When solutions were paid in tokens later exchanged for cash, claims rose to M = 9.4 (F(1, 447) = 47.62, p < .001), and 24 participants cheated maximally. — Mazar et al. PDF - Partial cheating across studies: "Across the six experiments (excluding the recycle + token condition), among the 791 participants who could cheat, we encountered only 5 (.6%) who cheated by the maximal amount… whereas most cheated only slightly." — Mazar et al. PDF - Replication: Verschuere et al. (2018), "Registered Replication Report on Mazar, Amir, and Ariely (2008)", AMPPS 1(3), 299–317, DOI 10.1177/2515245918781032. Original Experiment 1 had N = 229, a 1.45-matrix difference and d = 0.48. Across 25 direct replications (N = 5,786), the primary meta-analysis (19 labs, n = 4,674) found the moral reminder group reported 0.11 more matrices (95% CI [−0.09, 0.31]), d = −0.04, "numerically in the opposite direction". — Verschuere et al. 2018, PRIMARY-FULL - Integrity status: Crossref records an Expression of Concern on the article, published in JMR vol. 61, p. 1183 and dated 20 September 2024 (DOI 10.1177/00222437241285882). — Crossref record of the EoC. Retraction Watch (3 Sept 2026) describes it as an expression of concern over "the replicability of the results". — Retraction Watch, 3 Sep 2026. An informal forum summary says the notice also raises data-provenance concerns; I could not open the notice text to confirm this (SAGE returned 403). — Straight Dope forum, UNVERIFIED - Related Ariely and Gino cases: the 2012 PNAS paper by Shu, Mazar, Gino, Ariely & Bazerman (109, 15197–15200) was retracted on 13 Sept 2021 because Data Colada (post 98) "provided evidence questioning the validity of the data". — search summary of the PNAS notice; Harvard Crimson, 1 Sep 2021. Ariely's 2004 Psych Science paper received an EoC in July 2021, and Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002, Psych Science) was retracted 2 Sept 2026. — Retraction Watch 2026. Gino: three papers (2014 Psych Sci with Wiltermuth; 2015 Psych Sci with Kouchaki & Galinsky; 2020 JPSP with Kouchaki & Casciaro) were retracted in 2023, and Harvard revoked her tenure in May 2025. — Harvard Crimson, 27 May 2025

Shalvi, S., Dana, J., Handgraaf, M. J. J., & De Dreu, C. K. W. (2011). Justified ethicality: Observing desired counterfactuals modifies ethical perceptions and behavior. OBHDP, 115(2), 181–190. DOI 10.1016/j.obhdp.2011.02.001 [PRIMARY-ABSTRACT via IDEAS/RePEc] - In a die-under-cup task (private roll, pay by reported number), participants who rolled three times with only the first roll counting tended to report the highest of the three. Removing the chance to see more than one roll reduced lying. Seeing desired but non-paying counterfactual rolls also made lies seem less unethical. People value justifications that let them lie for money while still feeling honest. — IDEAS abstract page

Shalvi, S., Gino, F., Barkan, R., & Ayal, S. (2015). Self-serving justifications: Doing wrong and feeling moral. Current Directions in Psychological Science, 24(2), 125–130. DOI 10.1177/0963721414553264 [PRIMARY-ABSTRACT via Crossref; not retracted per Crossref] - "self-serving justifications emerging before and after people engage in intentional ethical violations mitigate the threat to the moral self, enabling them to do wrong while feeling moral. Pre-violation justifications lessen the anticipated threat… by redefining questionable behaviors as excusable. Post-violation justifications alleviate the experienced threat… through compensations that balance or lessen violations." — Crossref abstract

Fischbacher, U., & Föllmi-Heusi, F. (2013). Lies in disguise—An experimental study on cheating. Journal of the European Economic Association, 11(3), 525–547. DOI 10.1111/jeea.12014 [PRIMARY-FULL of the TWI working paper, version dated 25 Nov 2012] - Design: participants roll a die privately and report it. Payoffs are 1→1 CHF, 2→2, 3→3, 4→4, 5→5, 6→0. Individual lies cannot be detected; only the distribution can be checked. Baseline N = 389 inexperienced subjects. — TWI WP - Results: "about 20 percent of inexperienced subjects lie to the fullest extent possible while 39 percent of subjects are fully honest. In addition, a high share of subjects consists of partial liars; these subjects lie, but do not report the payoff-maximizing draw." 35.0% reported a 5 and 27.2% reported a 4 (both above the 16.7% expected), and 6.4% reported the zero-payoff 6, giving the 39% honesty estimate (6 × 6.4%). Income maximizers are at most 22%. "Significantly more than 1/6 of the subjects report 4." — TWI WP - Even in a no-die treatment where 5 CHF could be claimed outright, only 85% claimed 5 CHF and 4 of 34 claimed 4. — TWI WP - The authors attribute partial lying to reputational concerns: participants disguise lies from an outside observer. — search summary citing FFH, SECONDARY

Pooled evidence on partial dishonesty - Abeler, Nosenzo & Raymond (2019), "Preferences for truth-telling", Econometrica 87(4), 1115–1153, DOI 10.3982/ECTA14673. The published version pools 90 studies. The 2016 IZA working paper (72 studies, more than 32,000 subjects, 43 countries) reports an average standardized report of only 0.216: "subjects forgo on average about three-quarters of the potential gains from lying", a result robust to raising stakes 500-fold or repeating the decision up to 50 times. The published paper concludes that preferences for being seen as honest and for being honest are the main motives. — IZA DP 10188, PRIMARY-FULL WP; Econometric Society page - Gerlach, Teodorescu & Hertwig (2019), "The truth about lies: A meta-analysis on dishonest behavior", Psychological Bulletin 145(1), 1–44. It covers 565 experiments (44,050 choices) across sender-receiver, die-roll, coin-flip and matrix paradigms. A similar share of people lie in die-roll and matrix tasks, but liars in die-roll tasks lie to a much greater degree. There is substantial evidence of publication bias. — search summary of abstract and MPIB press release, PRIMARY-ABSTRACT/SECONDARY

Inferences

  • This is the strongest human precedent for the AI paper's main pattern. When people violate a standard they profess, the modal violation is partial. They take some of the gain but stop short of the maximum: Mazar's 0.6% maximal cheaters, Fischbacher's "report a 4", Abeler's three-quarters forgone. The theory says partial violation is what lets the self-image survive. A "middle" option is the multiple-choice version of reporting a 4.
  • Justification availability (Shalvi 2011 counterfactual rolls; Mazar's tokens) increases the size of the violation. If LLM compromise rates rise when the scenario offers a plausible rationale, that matches this mechanism.
  • Cite Mazar et al. (2008) for the partial-cheating pattern, which the field has replicated widely in die-roll meta-analyses. Do not cite it for the Ten Commandments effect, which failed the RRR. Always note the 2024 EoC.

Gaps

  • I could not open the Shalvi et al. (2011) full text, so its Ns and the exact percentage reporting the highest roll are not reported here.
  • The exact wording of the 2024 JMR Expression of Concern is unverified (publisher returned 403).

Q6. Moral licensing and moral credentials: Monin & Miller (2001); Sachdeva, Iliev & Medin (2009); Merritt, Effron & Monin (2010)

Takeaway

A prior good deed, or even an affirmed moral identity, gives people "credentials" or "credits" that free them to act in ways that could look immoral. The pooled effect is modest (d = 0.31 over 91 studies) and probably inflated by publication bias.

Cited Findings

Monin, B., & Miller, D. T. (2001). Moral credentials and the expression of prejudice. JPSP, 81(1), 33–43. DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.81.1.33 [PRIMARY-FULL, PDF hosted by the Notre Dame Lapsley lab] - Abstract: "In Study 1, participants given the opportunity to disagree with blatantly sexist statements were later more willing to favor a man for a stereotypically male job. In Study 2, participants who first had the opportunity to select a member of a stereotyped group (a woman or an African American) for a category-neutral job were more likely to reject a member of that group for a job stereotypically suited for majority members. In Study 3, participants who had established credentials as nonprejudiced persons revealed a greater willingness to express a politically incorrect opinion even when the audience was unaware of their credentials." — Monin & Miller PDF - Study 1 details: 202 Princeton undergraduates. The credential manipulation used "some" versus "most" wording of sexist statements, which made disagreement easier in the "most" condition (M = 4.15 versus 2.49 statements rejected). Male participants in the "most" (credentialed) condition favored a man more (M = 5.1) than those in the "some" (M = 4.4) and base-rate (M = 4.6) conditions. The effect did not appear for women. — Monin & Miller PDF

Sachdeva, S., Iliev, R., & Medin, D. L. (2009). Sinning saints and saintly sinners: The paradox of moral self-regulation. Psychological Science, 20(4), 523–528. DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02326.x [PRIMARY-ABSTRACT via Crossref] - "Participants who wrote a story referring to the positive traits donated one fifth as much as those who wrote a story referring to the negative traits." Experiment 2 showed the effect was due to a change in self-concept; Experiment 3 extended it to cooperation in environmental decision-making. "affirming a moral identity leads people to feel licensed to act immorally. However, when moral identity is threatened, moral behavior is a means to regain some lost self-worth." — Crossref abstract

Merritt, A. C., Effron, D. A., & Monin, B. (2010). Moral self-licensing: When being good frees us to be bad. Social and Personality Psychology Compass, 4(5), 344–357. DOI 10.1111/j.1751-9004.2010.00263.x [PRIMARY-ABSTRACT via Crossref] - The review covers licensing in political correctness, prosocial behavior and consumer choice. It names the open theoretical tensions: "Do good deeds reframe bad deeds (moral credentials) or merely balance them out (moral credits)? When does past behavior liberate and when does it constrain? Is self-licensing primarily for others' benefit (self-presentational) or is it also a way for people to reassure themselves…?" — Crossref abstract

Effron, D. A., & Conway, P. (2015). When virtue leads to villainy: Advances in research on moral self-licensing. Current Opinion in Psychology, 6, 32–35. DOI 10.1016/j.copsyc.2015.03.017 — citation verified via Crossref only; no abstract retrieved. — Crossref

Monin & Jordan: the chapter usually cited as "Monin & Jordan" is "The dynamic moral self: A social psychological perspective", in Personality, Identity, and Character (Narvaez & Lapsley, eds.), Cambridge University Press, 2009, pp. 341–354, DOI 10.1017/CBO9780511627125.016. That is 2009, not 2014. — Crossref record

Meta-analysis: Blanken, van de Ven & Zeelenberg (2015), PSPB 41(4), 540–558, DOI 10.1177/0146167215572134. 91 studies (7,397 participants), d = 0.31. Published studies show larger effects than unpublished ones, and no other moderator was supported. Later bias-corrected re-analyses argue the effect is inflated by publication bias. — Tilburg record and search summary

Inferences

  • Licensing is a temporal analogue to the AI pattern: the faithful analysis written first could work as a "credential" that licenses the compromise that follows. Monin & Miller's Study 3, where licensing operated even when the audience did not know the credentials, suggests the credential works on the self-image as well as on reputation.

Gaps

  • Effron & Conway (2015) content not retrieved. Sachdeva dollar amounts not retrieved (only "one fifth").

Q7. Ethical fading, bounded ethicality, moral disengagement and motivated moral reasoning as mechanisms

Takeaway

These frameworks explain how a person can endorse a standard yet violate it without feeling like a violator. In ethical fading, the ethical aspect of a decision drops out of view through euphemism and gradual escalation. In bounded ethicality, systematic and largely unconscious self-favoring limits on recognizing conflicts of interest affect the self but not one's view of others. In moral disengagement, cognitive restructuring switches off self-sanctions. In motivated moral reasoning, the desired conclusion shapes accountability judgments and even which normative principle gets applied.

Cited Findings

  • Tenbrunsel, A. E., & Messick, D. M. (2004). Ethical fading: The role of self-deception in unethical behavior. Social Justice Research, 17(2), 223–236. The abstract names four enablers of self-deception: language euphemisms, the slippery slope of decision-making, errors in perceptual causation, and constraints induced by representations of the self. It argues that ethics training alone is likely to have limited effect. — ProQuest abstract via search summary, PRIMARY-ABSTRACT
  • Chugh, D., Bazerman, M. H., & Banaji, M. R. (2005). Bounded ethicality as a psychological barrier to recognizing conflicts of interest. In Moore, Cain, Loewenstein & Bazerman (eds.), Conflicts of Interest, Cambridge University Press, pp. 74–95. [PRIMARY-FULL, author manuscript] "Bounded rationality refers to the limits on the quality of general decision-making, and bounded ethicality is a strand that is used to refer to the limits on the quality of decision-making with ethical import… individuals view themselves as moral, competent, and deserving, and this view obstructs their ability to see and recognize conflicts of interest when they occur. Thus, ethicality is not bounded in unpredictable or non-systematic ways, but in systematic ways that unconsciously favor this particular vision of the self… we do not challenge the individual's capacity to recognize conflicts of interest in the abstract, or in the situations facing others, but rather in the situations involving the self." — Chugh et al. manuscript (NYU). Chugh & Kern (2016) later call this the "1.0" model and propose a 2.0 version. — Chugh 2016 ROB, SECONDARY
  • Bandura, A. (1999). Moral disengagement in the perpetration of inhumanities. Personality and Social Psychology Review, 3(3), 193–209. The mechanisms usually listed are moral justification, euphemistic labeling, advantageous comparison, displacement of responsibility, diffusion of responsibility, disregard or distortion of consequences, dehumanization and attribution of blame. They are grouped by locus: the conduct, the agency, and the outcomes or victims. — summary of mechanism list, SECONDARY; core.ac.uk full-text record (not opened)
  • Ditto, P. H., Pizarro, D. A., & Tannenbaum, D. (2009). Motivated moral reasoning. In Psychology of Learning and Motivation, Vol. 50 (Moral Judgment and Decision Making), pp. 307–338. DOI 10.1016/S0079-7421(08)00410-6. Central claim: some moral judgments that look objective are "more about motivated reasoning in service of some desired conclusion". Motivation works through two pathways: (1) perceptions of the actor's moral accountability and (2) the normative principles people use to judge the act. — bibliographic record and abstract via search summary, PRIMARY-ABSTRACT. A companion paper is Uhlmann, Pizarro, Tannenbaum & Ditto (2009), "The motivated use of moral principles", JDM 4(6), 479–491. — EconPapers

Inferences

  • Diffusion of responsibility (Bandura) is what the DWK multiple-dictator treatment and Hamman et al.'s delegation design operationalize. Euphemistic labeling and advantageous comparison are the rhetorical counterpart of a "middle" option, which can be framed as "balanced" or "measured" next to the bad option.
  • The Chugh et al. self-only boundary matches the Valdesolo own-versus-other asymmetry: the standard is recognized in the abstract and in others, but not applied to one's own case.
  • Ditto et al.'s second pathway, motivation choosing which principle applies, is the mechanism to cite if LLM action rationales switch to a different principle than the one their own analysis named.

Gaps

  • I did not open the full texts of Bazerman & Tenbrunsel, Blind Spots (Princeton UP, 2011), Tenbrunsel & Messick (2004), Bandura (1999) or Ditto et al. (2009). Statements about them rest on abstracts and secondary summaries.

Q8. Hypocrisy as compromise or partial violation; stated values versus behavior under situational pressure

Takeaway

Several paradigms show violations that are graded rather than all-or-none: partial lying, randomizing instead of choosing, revising "fair" to fit one's choice, and keeping more when acting through others. The broader values–behavior literature shows that stated values predict behavior unevenly, and least well for values that carry strong normative pressure. That is the situation in which stated priorities and actions most easily come apart.

Cited Findings

  • Partial violation (compiled from Q4 and Q5): only 0.6% of 791 cheaters cheated maximally — Mazar et al. 2008; 27.2% reported the non-maximal "4" against 16.7% expected — Fischbacher & Föllmi-Heusi WP; about three-quarters of potential lying gains forgone across 72 studies — Abeler et al. WP; 24% of dictators let a random device choose between the fair and unfair options — Dana et al. 2007.
  • Compromise as explicit theory: Rustichini & Villeval call stated fairness "a compromise with power" and hypocrisy "the attempt… to strike a balance between self-image and immediate convenience". — IZA DP 6590. Mazar et al. predict dishonesty "up to a certain level that does not force them to update their self-concept". — Mazar et al.
  • Values–behavior link (Bardi & Schwartz 2003): Bardi, A., & Schwartz, S. H. (2003). Values and behavior: Strength and structure of relations. PSPB, 29(10), 1207–1220, DOI 10.1177/0146167203254602. Stimulation and tradition values relate strongly to the behaviors that express them; hedonism, power, universalism and self-direction moderately; security, conformity, achievement and benevolence only marginally. The authors suggest the weaker links may come from normative pressures to perform certain behaviors regardless of one's values. — abstract via search summary of the Sage/HUJI records, PRIMARY-ABSTRACT
  • Value–action gap (Kollmuss & Agyeman 2002): "Mind the gap: Why do people act environmentally and what are the barriers to pro-environmental behavior?" Environmental Education Research, 8(3), 239–260, DOI 10.1080/13504620220145401. The paper rejects early linear models (knowledge → awareness/concern → action) because quantitative research shows an attitude–behavior gap. It analyzes demographic, external (institutional, economic, social, cultural) and internal (motivation, knowledge, values, attitudes, locus of control and similar) factors, and holds that no single framework captures pro-environmental behavior. — search summary of course PDF and abstract listings, SECONDARY
  • Professed versus private judgment when one's own interests are at stake: Polman & Ruttan (2012) found that the self-versus-other gap in what people say others "should" donate versus what they would give holds with real money, and that guilt eliminates it. — Crossref abstract

Inferences

  • Taken together, the human literature predicts compromise-type violations: when a stated standard meets self-interest or situational pressure, people find the action that keeps self-image and payoff both partly intact. The AI finding that models choose "middle" options more often than "obviously bad" ones matches this, so the moral-hypocrisy framing is apt for the pattern. Whether LLMs have anything like self-image motives is a separate question this literature cannot answer.
  • Bardi & Schwartz's finding that benevolence and conformity, the most normatively loaded values, have the weakest value–behavior links warns against treating any stated priority as a reliable predictor of action, for people or for models.
  • Keep the two framings separate, as the repo's CLAUDE.md requires. These paradigms measure the gap between a person's own professed standard and their own behavior, which corresponds to within-agent consistency. They do not measure which normative theory the behavior resembles.

Gaps

  • I found no human study that explicitly offers a three-option menu (fair / middle / selfish) and reports a preference for the middle as hypocrisy. The closest are DWK's random-cutoff treatment and partial reporting in die-roll tasks. A targeted search, for example of "compromise effect" in ethical choice or "moderate dishonesty", could still find one.
  • Bardi & Schwartz correlation coefficients and the Kollmuss & Agyeman full text were not retrieved.
