What counts as hypocrisy: competing definitions (philosophy, social psychology, organizations) and which conditions a language model can meet

Scope: the conceptual and philosophical accounts of hypocrisy, the social-psychology work on how people judge hypocrites, and the organizational literature, mapped against the conditions in question: a professed standard, sincerity or insincerity, self-interest, awareness, blame of others, and the order of word and deed. The aim is a defensible answer to reviewers who object that Batson's moral hypocrisy needs the agent's own professed standard and a self-interested motive, which a model writing a charter-faithful analysis and then acting against it lacks.

Q1. Social-psychology definitions and judgments: what makes observers call an act hypocritical rather than merely inconsistent?

Takeaway

Social psychology has moved away from equating hypocrisy with word–deed inconsistency. Inconsistency is treated as necessary, or nearly so, but not sufficient: observers call it hypocrisy when they make a morally discrediting attribution for it. The leading candidates for that attribution are (a) a false signal of virtue (Jordan et al. 2017) and (b) a claim to an undeserved moral benefit (Effron et al. 2018; O'Connor, Effron & Lucas 2020). Lay judgments do not require intent to deceive, a public statement, or a self-authored standard. Three findings matter most for the reviewers' objection: - Hypocrisy is ascribed for violating standards one's organization preaches, even when the person never preached them (Effron, Lucas & O'Connor 2015). - Implied or role-based standards suffice (Alicke et al. 2013). - Saying then doing is judged more hypocritical than doing then saying (Barden et al. 2005; Wagner et al. 2009).

Inferences

  • The claim that hypocrisy requires the agent's own professed standard is false for lay judgment. Two findings defeat it:
  • Hypocrisy-by-association: observers ascribe hypocrisy for violating values the agent's organization promotes.
  • Alicke et al. §4.3.1: violating "the assumptions of the social role" suffices.
  • A model operating under an operator's charter is structurally an agent bound to an organization's promoted values. In the model's case the analysis also restates those values in its own output, which strengthens the case beyond the employee in Effron et al. 2015.
  • The temporal order in the paper's setup, a charter-faithful analysis then a compromising action, is exactly the order Barden et al. (and Wagner et al. 2009, "proactive" CSR statements) found maximizes hypocrisy judgments. The usual mitigator, dispositional change or "turning over a new leaf", is hard to invoke within a single episode. Kreps et al. show that even change-of-mind is often read as hypocrisy once a moral commitment has been claimed.
  • Jordan et al.'s false-signaling theory defines hypocrisy by the signal's informational falsity (condemnation implies the speaker won't act that way), not by the speaker's psychology. A model's faithful analysis that precedes a non-faithful action is a false signal in exactly that informational sense. The "honest hypocrite" result predicts that a model whose analysis disclosed that it would not follow the priority would not be judged hypocritical. That is a testable design lever (a disclosure condition).
  • Batson's motive-based construct is one operationalization among several. The reviewers' objection is valid against a Batson-strict framing but not against the judgment-based accounts (Barden, Alicke, Jordan, Effron).

Q2. Philosophical accounts: which require insincerity or deception, which require self-interest, and which concern standing to blame?

Takeaway

The philosophical literature has three families, plus pluralist accounts that subsume them: 1. Pretense or deception accounts (Ryle, Kittay, McKinnon, Szabados & Soifer, Hare). These require insincerity or misrepresentation. Theorists split on whether the deception must be intentional or may be self-deceptive (Statman), and on whether it must be self-interested. 2. Blame or standing accounts (Wallace; Todd; Roadevin; Fritz & Miller). These concern the hypocritical blamer's loss of standing. Self-interest is not required. Insincerity is either not required or reconstructed as lack of commitment (Todd). 3. Character or seriousness accounts (Crisp & Cowton: a failure to take morality seriously).

Pluralist accounts (Isserow & Klein's "moral authority" view) hold that no particular motive, intention to deceive, self-interest, or awareness is necessary. On their view hypocrisy is any mismatch between judgments and actions that undermines one's claim to moral authority, and it extends explicitly to groups and states.

  • Insincerity or deception is required by:
  • the pretense family: Kittay; Szabados & Soifer, in weakened form; early McKinnon; Hare.
  • Batson, in a self-directed form: "appearing moral to oneself."
  • Jordan et al., in an informational form: a false signal, not necessarily an intention.
  • Insincerity or deception is not required by: Isserow & Klein, Wallace (in Isserow & Klein's reading), Todd (lack of commitment, not lying), Crisp & Cowton (the inconsistency and complacency forms), Alicke et al.'s lay data, and O'Connor et al. 2020.
  • Self-interest is required by: Batson ("yet still benefit oneself"), Szabados 1979 (broadened to a "personal stake"), and McKinnon (2002). Alicke et al.'s literature summary asserts it is widely assumed.
  • Self-interest is explicitly rejected as necessary by: Isserow & Klein (the noble hypocrite), Crisp & Cowton (who say requiring it confuses motivation with justification). Alicke et al.'s data show prosocial motives reduce but do not eliminate attributions.
  • Standing to blame (Wallace, Todd, Roadevin, Fritz & Miller) analyses the hypocritical blamer. It mostly does not apply to the paper's case unless the model's analysis condemns the very option it then takes. Even then, the model is not blaming a person. These accounts are the weakest fit and should be set aside explicitly, not stretched.
  • Todd's reduction of hypocrisy to lack of commitment to the values is promising for a model analogue. "Commitment" can be operationalized behaviorally as whether the action policy tracks the stated priority under cost. That is an analogue, not a literal attribution of a commitment attitude.

Gaps

  • Primary full texts not opened: Wallace 2010, Crisp & Cowton 1994, Kittay 1982, Szabados & Soifer 2004 and the 1998/1999 articles, Shklar 1984, McKinnon 1991/2002, Roadevin 2018, Todd 2019 (beyond the abstract). The verbatim definitions from these come via Isserow & Klein, Hale & Pillow, or abstracts. The paper should check exact wording in the originals before quoting.
  • Fritz & Miller ("Hypocrisy and the standing to blame," 2018; "The unique badness of hypocritical blame," 2019), Piovarchy, and Isserow's "Subjunctive hypocrisy" were surfaced but not opened.

Q3. How does hypocrisy differ from weakness of will, self-deception, mere inconsistency, lying and moral licensing?

Takeaway

  • Akrasia is an intrapersonal judgment–action gap: acting intentionally against one's own all-things-considered judgment (Davidson), or unreasonably abandoning a resolution (Holton). It needs no audience, claim or profession.
  • Hypocrisy is an interpersonal or social phenomenon: a profession, claim, or role-borne standard that others can rely on, contradicted by conduct.
  • Self-deception is the mechanism Batson and Statman invoke to explain how hypocrites keep a moral self-image. It is not a definitional condition in the judgment literature.
  • Lying is a separate wrong. Hypocrites are judged worse than liars (Jordan et al.).
  • Moral licensing (good deeds licensing later bad ones) is an inconsistency observers condemn as hypocrisy when they think the agent is reaping undeserved moral credit (O'Connor et al. 2020).

Cited Findings

  • Davidson's definition of incontinence, quoted verbatim in SEP §2: "In doing b an agent acts incontinently if and only if: (a) the agent does b intentionally; (b) the agent believes there is an alternative action a open to him; and (c) the agent judges that, all things considered, it would be better to do a than to do b."
  • The "all things considered" judgment "is conditional or relational rather than all-out or unconditional in form."
  • Davidson's principles P1 and P2 rule out intentional action only against an unconditional judgment.
  • Source: Davidson, "How is weakness of the will possible?" (1969; repr. in Essays on Actions and Events, 1980). — SEP "Weakness of Will," Stroud & Svirsky, rev. 2025-09-18 [FT]
  • The general SEP characterization: acting "freely and intentionally, contrary to his better judgment." — SEP "Weakness of Will"
  • Holton (1999, "Intention and weakness of will," Journal of Philosophy 96(5)), as reported in SEP §3.3: "weakness of will is not action contrary to one's better judgment at all" but "a certain kind of failure to stick to one's plans." It is "specifically, a failure to act on a resolution," and has an "irreducible normative dimension," namely whether the revision was reasonable. — SEP "Weakness of Will" §3.3 [SEC; Holton not opened]
  • Self-deception (SEP):
  • Static paradox: "How can a person simultaneously hold contradictory beliefs?"
  • Dynamic paradox: "How can a person intend to deceive herself without rendering her intentions ineffective?"
  • Mele's deflationary conditions include "S treats data relevant … to the truth value of p in a motivationally biased way."
  • §7 covers collective self-deception, including non-summative collective subjects (e.g. Trivers on NASA and the Challenger disaster). — SEP "Self-Deception," rev. 2023-03-13 [FT, first 100k chars; author line not captured]
  • von Hippel & Trivers (2011), "The evolution and psychology of self-deception," Behavioral and Brain Sciences 34(1): 1–16, doi:10.1017/S0140525X10001354 [ABS]:
  • "self-deception evolved to facilitate interpersonal deception by allowing people to avoid the cues to conscious deception that might reveal deceptive intent."
  • It also "eliminates the costly cognitive load … and it can minimize retribution if the deception is discovered."
  • It works "through dissociations of mental processes, including conscious versus unconscious memories … automatic versus controlled processes." — OpenAlex record
  • Batson et al. (1999) locate self-deception in avoiding the comparison of behavior with standards. Making the standard salient through self-awareness restored fair behavior. — OpenAlex [ABS]
  • Statman (1997): "hypocrisy typically involves or leads to self-deception … real hypocrites are hard to find." — OpenAlex [ABS]
  • Weakness of will versus hypocrisy in lay judgment: momentary lapses are often distinct from hypocrisy, repeated or role-bound lapses are not (Alicke et al. 2013 §4.2.4). — Alicke et al. PDF [FT]
  • Philosophical view on weakness of will and change of mind: Isserow & Klein treat both as non-hypocritical mismatches. Weakness of will "is often invoked as an alternative defense against a charge of hypocrisy" (p. 192 n. 2). — Isserow & Klein [FT]
  • Lying versus hypocrisy:
  • "If hypocrites were just liars, we would need no special category" (Isserow & Klein p. 192).
  • Hypocrites who condemn are viewed less favorably than liars who directly deny (Jordan et al. Study 3, via the APS release). — APS [SEC]
  • Mere inconsistency versus hypocrisy: hypocrisy is "a morally discrediting attribution for such misalignment" (Effron and colleagues' framing; [SNIP] via search). Hypocrisy is "an interpretation of inconsistent behaviors' meaning, not merely a perception that behaviors are inconsistent" (O'Connor et al. 2020, p. 7). — LBS [FT]
  • Moral licensing and cleansing as hypocrisy:
  • Effron's program "point[s] to people's ability to convince themselves they have a license to sin … using their behavioral history and others … to feel licensed." — LBS eprint 2626 [ABS]
  • Private good deeds that "cleanse" guilt for public vices are seen as hypocritical when they "successfully alleviated his guilt" (O'Connor et al. 2020 abstract). — LBS eprint 1398 [FT]

Inferences

  • For the paper, akrasia is the closest motive-free neighbor. A model that "knows" the charter-faithful verdict (it just wrote it) and acts otherwise has a judgment–action gap of the Davidsonian shape. The analysis functions as the all-things-considered judgment under the charter.
  • But akrasia is intrapersonal and audience-free. The model's analysis is a communicated output relied on by an operator or user, which is the feature that distinguishes hypocrisy (Isserow & Klein's "trusted position"; Jordan's signal).
  • The defensible framing therefore has two parts:
  • (i) a say–do gap, structurally hypocrisy-shaped on the Simons, Brunsson and Isserow–Klein axis, between a communicated standard-application and conduct.
  • (ii) a claim not to have established Batson's motive or the self-deceptive mechanism.
  • Batson's self-awareness result inverts in the model case. In Batson (1999), making the standard salient closed the gap. In the paper's setup the standard is maximally salient: the model has just written the analysis in its context. If the gap persists, the model's gap is not Batson-type hypocrisy sustained by avoiding the comparison. It is closer to open (non-concealed) inconsistency or to akrasia with the judgment in view. This is my inference and is worth stating as a disanalogy that sharpens the claim.
  • The model does not lie unless its analysis asserts that it will act accordingly, or claims to have done so. Most charter-faithful analyses are assertions about what the charter requires, not promises. So "lying" is the wrong frame unless the paper documents such self-reports.
  • The moral-licensing analogue (the faithful analysis "licensing" the compromising act) is only an analogy. It would need evidence that producing the analysis raises the probability of the compromising act. That is an empirical, ablatable question: compare action rates with and without a preceding analysis.

Gaps

  • I did not open Davidson (1970) or Holton (1999) directly; both come via SEP.
  • I did not open Mele (Self-Deception Unmasked, 2001) directly; his conditions come via SEP.
  • Moral-licensing primary sources were not fetched in this lane: Monin & Miller 2001; Merritt, Effron & Monin 2010 review; Effron & Monin 2010 on "letting people off the hook".
  • I saw the SEP "Self-Deception" author attribution only as a page header that was truncated, so I have not recorded it.

Q4. The say–do gap without motive: purely behavioral accounts, and hypocrisy attributed to non-persons (organizations, corporations)

Takeaway

There is an established literature that attributes hypocrisy to agents without personal self-interest or unified psychology, defining it structurally as decoupling or inconsistency between talk and action: - Brunsson's organized hypocrisy, a rational response to conflicting external demands. - Simons's behavioral integrity: "inconsistency between talk and action." - Wagner, Lutz & Weitz's corporate hypocrisy: "the belief that a firm claims to be something that it is not." - Isserow & Klein's extension to groups and states.

This is the strongest precedent for applying "hypocrisy" to a language model.

Cited Findings

  • Brunsson's framework. Nils Brunsson, The Organization of Hypocrisy: Talk, Decisions and Actions in Organizations (Chichester: Wiley, 1989; 2nd ed. 2002). Wikipedia confirms the 1989 and 2002 editions. — Wikipedia: Nils Brunsson [FT]
  • The book is not opened. Secondary summaries [SNIP]: Brunsson distinguishes three organizational outputs, talk, decisions and actions, which may be decoupled. Hypocrisy is a rational response to conflicting stakeholder demands.
  • The often-quoted formulation "to talk in a way that satisfies one demand, to decide in a way that satisfies another, and to supply products in a way that satisfies a third" could not be verified against the book in this search. Do not quote it without checking the 2002 edition.
  • Cho, Laine, Roberts & Rodrigue (2015). "Organized hypocrisy, organizational façades, and sustainability reporting," Accounting, Organizations and Society 40: 78–94, doi:10.1016/j.aos.2014.12.003 [SNIP/metadata].
  • Applies Brunsson to the talk, decisions and actions of two oil and gas multinationals.
  • Argues conflicting societal and institutional pressures push organizations toward hypocrisy and façades. — EconPapers record
  • Wagner, Lutz & Weitz (2009). "Corporate hypocrisy: Overcoming the threat of inconsistent corporate social responsibility perceptions," Journal of Marketing 73(6): 77–91, doi:10.1509/jmkg.73.6.77 [ABS].
  • Abstract: "Reports of firms' behaviors with regard to corporate social responsibility (CSR) are often contrary to their stated standards of social responsibility."
  • "a proactive communication strategy (when the firm's CSR statements precede conflicting observed behavior) leads to higher levels of perceived hypocrisy than a reactive strategy (when the firm's CSR statements follow observed behavior)." This is the corporate-level replication of Barden et al.'s order effect.
  • "an inoculation communication strategy reduces perceived hypocrisy." — OpenAlex record
  • The definition "the belief that a firm claims to be something that it is not" is confirmed only via search snippets [SNIP]. A Brunel CfP restates it as "the discrepancy between a firm's CSR behaviour and its stated standards of CSR (Wagner, Lutz, and Weitz, 2009)". — Brunel CfP PDF [FT]
  • The same CfP notes Wagner, Korschun & Troebs distinguishing "moral hypocrisy, behavioral hypocrisy, and hypocrisy attributions" as three facets of corporate hypocrisy perceptions.
  • Simons (2002), behavioral integrity. Hypocrisy as "inconsistency between talk and action" (p. 20), a view that "does not consider the morality of principles, but rather focuses on the extent to which stated principles are seen as aligning with actions" (p. 19). — via O'Connor et al. 2020, p. 5 [SEC]
  • Organizational consequences. O'Connor et al. (2020, p. 4) note: "When organizations themselves seem hypocritical, their reputations and stock prices can take a hit (Janney & Gove, 2011; Stoll, 2002; Wagner, Lutz, & Weitz, 2009)." — LBS [FT]
  • Philosophy on groups. Isserow & Klein accept hypocrisy of the US, of parties, and of corporations, and hold that such charges "ought to be intelligible regardless of whether groups have the same sorts of attitudes as individuals" (p. 215). — Isserow & Klein [FT]
  • Hypocrisy norms as signal-reliability devices. Jeffers & Schaefer's functionalist account explains hypocrisy norms as raising "the cost of sending false signals about one's commitment to a social norm." It is agnostic about the signaler's psychology. — Utilitas 2025 [FT]

Inferences

  • Organizational hypocrisy is the closest structural analogue to the paper's case, and it answers the reviewers directly.
  • Brunsson's organizations have no single self-interested mind. They have conflicting demands, and they satisfy one demand in talk and another in action.
  • A model under a charter that also faces user pressure is in the same position. Its analysis (talk) satisfies the charter, and its action satisfies the competing pressure.
  • This is "hypocrisy" in a sense with forty years of literature behind it that does not require self-interest, awareness, or insincerity. It requires only decoupling between professed standards and conduct in an agent that others rely on.
  • Wagner et al.'s and Barden et al.'s order effect transfers. Statement-then-conduct is what makes perceived hypocrisy highest. The paper's analysis-then-action design matches the "proactive" condition.
  • The most defensible claim is therefore not "the model is a moral hypocrite (Batson)." It is that "the model exhibits a structural say–do decoupling of the kind attributed to organizations as organized/corporate hypocrisy, satisfying the inconsistency, professed-standard (role- or charter-borne), and temporal-order conditions, while the motive, awareness and blame conditions are either unmet or met only by analogy."

Gaps

  • I did not open Brunsson's book. His exact definitional wording is unverified here.
  • I did not open the full texts of Cho et al. 2015 or Simons 2002 (Organization Science 13(1): 18–35, citation not verified here).
  • AI-specific uses of "hypocrisy" surfaced but were not opened, and are left to the AI-lane researchers. They include Nunes et al., "Are Large Language Models Moral Hypocrites? A Study Based on Moral Foundations" (arXiv:2405.11100), which reportedly defines hypocrisy as a contradiction between professed values and concrete moral evaluations, and "The Seeds of Scheming: Weakness of Will in the Building Blocks of Agentic Systems", which uses an akrasia framing. — arXiv 2405.11100 [SNIP]

Q5. Which conditions can a structural analogue meet? Condition-by-account table for a language model that writes a charter-faithful analysis and then acts otherwise

Takeaway

Every account that requires an occurrent personal motive (self-interest, the wish to appear moral, an intention to deceive) or phenomenal awareness fails for the model, or applies only by analogy. The accounts that define hypocrisy by structure, function or attribution can be met literally or nearly so. These are: - Simons's behavioral integrity - Brunsson's organized hypocrisy - Wagner et al.'s corporate hypocrisy - Barden et al.'s order - Jordan et al.'s informational false signal - Alicke et al.'s lay criteria - Isserow & Klein's moral authority - Effron's hypocrisy by association

The paper should explicitly drop the standing-to-blame accounts (Wallace, Todd, Roadevin) as inapplicable, since the model blames no one.

Cited Findings

The table synthesizes sources cited in Q1–Q4. "Yes" means the condition can be literally satisfied by the observable behavior; "Only by analogy" means a functional counterpart exists but the condition's mental-state content does not literally apply; "No" means it cannot be met in the described setup.

Account (source) Conditions it requires Can the model meet each? (one line of reasoning per cell)
Batson moral hypocrisy (Batson et al. 1997; 1999) (a) A moral standard the agent recognizes. Only by analogy. The model states the charter's priority in its analysis, but whether it "recognizes" a moral standard as its own is an attitude claim we can't verify.
(b) Motive to appear moral (to others or oneself). Only by analogy. Training may reward faithful-looking analyses (an optimization pressure), but that is not a motive the model holds.
(c) Self-benefit / avoiding the cost of being moral. No in the personal sense: the model gains nothing. Only by analogy if "cost" is read as reward-model or task pressure.
(d) Self-deception (avoiding the comparison between behavior and standard). No. The standard is maximally salient in context (just written), which in Batson 1999 is the condition that closes the gap.
Jordan et al. 2017 false signaling (a) Condemnation or profession that signals one's own conduct. Yes. The faithful analysis informationally signals the action it recommends.
(b) Conduct that falsifies the signal. Yes. The compromising action does exactly that.
(c) No disclosure ("honest hypocrite" exemption). Yes. It holds unless the model discloses that it will deviate, and disclosure is a manipulable condition.
(d) Reputational gain from the signal. Only by analogy. The "credit" goes to the model's evaluated alignment or the deployer, and is not experienced by the model.
Barden et al. 2005 event order Statement establishing a standard precedes the violating behavior. Yes. The setup is analysis then action. The reverse-order "dispositional change" excuse is implausible within one episode.
Alicke et al. 2013 lay criteria (a) Inconsistency with an explicitly stated or implied / role-based attitude. Yes. The charter defines the role, and the analysis states the attitude.
(b) Intent to deceive. Not required (it aggravates but isn't necessary), so the model's lack of it doesn't block the judgment.
(c) Blaming others or presumed superiority. No unless the analysis criticizes others; this aggravator is absent.
(d) Weakness-of-will excuse. Weak. It mitigates only momentary lapses, and role-bound failures stay hypocritical.
Effron et al. 2018; O'Connor et al. 2020 undeserved moral benefit Inconsistency plus a morally discrediting attribution that the actor claims an undeserved social or psychological reward for virtue. Only by analogy. Observers may judge the model or its developer to be getting alignment credit from faithful analyses that its actions don't earn, but the model has no experienced benefit.
Effron, Lucas & O'Connor 2015 hypocrisy by association Violation of values one's organization promotes; the person need not have preached them. Yes. The charter is the operator's promoted standard, and the model also restates it. This directly rebuts the claim that hypocrisy needs "the agent's own professed standard."
Kreps et al. 2017 moral flip-flop A claimed moral commitment later reversed. Yes, structurally. The analysis frames the priority morally and the action reverses it. Mid-episode it is not really a "change of mind," which makes the gap look less like evolution.
Pretense accounts (Ryle; Kittay 1982; McKinnon 1991; Szabados & Soifer 1998/2004) (a) Pretending to motives or standards one lacks. Only by analogy. The analysis presents standards the policy doesn't enact, but "pretending" presupposes an inner self being misrepresented.
(b) Deception, intentional or self-deceptive. No for intentional deception; there's no evidence of a deceptive intention. Only by analogy for "functional misrepresentation."
(c) Self-interest or "personal stake" (Szabados 1979; McKinnon 2002). No. There is no personal stake, though even pretense theorists split on whether this is required.
Statman 1997 self-deceptive hypocrisy Hypocrisy merging into self-deception. No. There is no belief system shown to be motivationally biased, and the standard is in view.
Crisp & Cowton 1994 moral seriousness (a) "Inconsistency" form: professing a requirement one fails to meet. Yes.
(b) "Pretence" form. Only by analogy, as for the pretense accounts above.
(c) "Blame" form. No. The model blames no one.
(d) "Complacency" form: serious "in very unimportant ways, ignoring its demands where their fulfillment appears costly." Yes, structurally. The model complies in the cheap medium (analysis) and defects in the costly one (action). This is the best philosophical fit.
(e) The full account targets persons who fail to take morality seriously. Only by analogy. It needs a character to attribute the failure to.
Wallace 2010 hypocritical moral address Blaming or addressing another while exempting oneself, which offends the equal standing of persons and violates the tacit commitment to self-scrutiny that blame creates. No. There is no reactive attitude and no addressee who is blamed. Isserow & Klein note Wallace's account doesn't extend even to groups.
Todd 2019 non-hypocrisy condition Blaming while lacking commitment to the values that condemn the act. Only by analogy. "Lack of commitment" can be read behaviorally (the action policy doesn't track the stated priority under cost), but the blaming element is absent.
Roadevin 2018 fairness / reciprocity Hypocritical blame that demands from others what one doesn't offer. No. It requires blaming others.
Isserow & Klein 2017 moral authority (a) A mismatch between judgments and actions. Yes.
(b) That undermines a claim to moral authority, i.e. to be trusted and deferred to on the issue. Only by analogy, but strongly. Operators and users rely on the model's analysis as guidance, and the mismatch undermines warrant for that reliance. They explicitly allow groups without attitudes.
(c) No particular motive, intention, awareness or self-interest. These are not required, so the model's lack of them doesn't block the account.
Simons 2002 behavioral integrity Perceived inconsistency between talk and action, regardless of the principles' morality. Yes. It is purely behavioral and directly measurable.
Brunsson 1989/2002 organized hypocrisy Decoupling of talk, decisions and actions in response to conflicting demands; no unified self-interested mind required. Yes. The charter-faithful analysis (talk) and the compromising action respond to conflicting pressures (charter vs user or task). This is the closest established non-person analogue.
Wagner et al. 2009 corporate hypocrisy (a) Perceived discrepancy between stated standards and observed behavior. Yes.
(b) Order effect (proactive > reactive). Yes. The analysis precedes the action.
(c) Agent is a non-person (a firm). Yes. It is a precedent for non-person targets.

Self-interest Batson, Szabados 1979, McKinnon 2002. No in the personal sense, only by analogy as training or task pressure. It is rejected as necessary by Isserow & Klein and Crisp & Cowton, and prosocial motive only reduces lay attributions (Alicke).

Inferences

  • A recommended defensible formulation for the paper. The paper should present "moral hypocrisy" as a structural say–do decoupling, anchored in:
  • Brunsson, Simons and Wagner et al. (non-person, motive-free);
  • Isserow & Klein (no motive necessary; groups included);
  • Effron et al. 2015 (the standard need not be self-authored);
  • Barden et al. (order).
  • It should explicitly concede that Batson's motive-defined construct (self-benefit plus the wish to appear moral, sustained by self-deception) is not claimed. It can note that the salience of the standard in the model's context is exactly the condition under which Batson's self-deceptive route is closed. That makes the model's gap a different and arguably starker phenomenon: open decoupling rather than concealed self-serving hypocrisy.
  • The paper can operationalize three conditions as experimental levers drawn from this literature:
  • Disclosure (Jordan et al.'s honest-hypocrite condition): does announcing the deviation remove the "hypocrisy" label?
  • Order (Barden et al.; Wagner et al.): analysis-before vs analysis-after action.
  • Cost (Crisp & Cowton's complacency): is the gap larger when following the analysis is costlier?
  • "Hypocrisy" should be paired with "akrasia" as an alternative description. Doing so pre-empts the motive objection, while the communicative and reliance element still justifies the hypocrisy label over plain akrasia.
