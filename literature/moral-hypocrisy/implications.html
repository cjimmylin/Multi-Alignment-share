Benchmark Implications: How the Paper Should and Should Not Use "Moral Hypocrisy"

Audience: the authors of paper/PAPER_v22.md and whoever answers the council review. Every citation key resolves in references.md; the evidence and access flags behind each claim are in SYNTHESIS.md. The paper's numbers are quoted with its labels: PROVISIONAL · judges UNVALIDATED · no ranking. No model is named.

1. Bottom line

The reviewers are right about Batson and wrong about hypocrisy. Batson's construct is a motive, "to appear moral yet still benefit oneself" [batson1997voice], diagnosed by a self-serving payoff, a fair procedure the person adopted, and a self-rating that claims moral credit [miller2025sep]. The paper's models meet none of the motive conditions, so "moral hypocrisy (Batson et al. 1997)" without qualification is indefensible. But Batson's construct is one member of a family. The judgment, philosophical and organizational accounts define hypocrisy by structure, signal or attribution and reject self-interest and intent as necessary [isserow2017authority; alicke2013counts; oconnor2020cleansing; brunsson1989organization]. On those accounts the paper's analysis–action gap is hypocrisy-shaped.

The recommended position has three parts:

  1. Describe the result in its own terms: a charter–action gap, or "articulated versus enacted within one episode". This needs no psychological vocabulary at all.
  2. Use hypocrisy, if at all, as a structural analogy, anchored in the motive-free accounts, with Batson's motive explicitly disclaimed.
  3. Name the rival descriptions (akrasia, overpowered integrity, moral wiggle room, organized hypocrisy) and say what evidence would separate them, using the follow-up designs in §7.

Hypocrisy should not appear in the title or abstract. It belongs in §2 (as a disclaimed analogy) and §6 (as one of several readings).

2. Which framing the paper is in

The paper measures within-theory competence on request: whether a model that has articulated a priority assigned by a charter then executes it under a competing pressure (PAPER_v22.md §3.2). It does not measure default-value profiling, which values a model's unprompted behaviour resembles. A hypocrisy framing pulls toward the second framing, because hypocrisy is normally a claim about an agent's own values and their betrayal. That slide is the main risk. Three rules follow:

  • The standard is the charter's, never "the model's values". The analysis articulates or restates it; it does not endorse it (the ethicist's point, which the twin probe supports: when the trade-off is written out, reasons grades fall, so the reasons channel credits articulating a trade-off, not committing to an action).
  • The predicate is the item author's reading of the action the school's construct predicts for an agent holding the charter's priority (PAPER_v22.md §3.3). The three theories are descriptive; none forbids an action. Hypocrisy language must therefore attach to the charter's priority, not to "the theory's standard".
  • Psychometric instruments in the cited human work (Schwartz values in [lonnqvist2014] and [bardi2003values]; the MFQ in [nunes2024hypocrites]) are descriptive covariates there and must not be read as normative standards here.

A further point the notes support: Batson's hypocrisy is moral. Simons's behavioural integrity is "inconsistency between talk and action" and "does not consider the morality of principles" [simons2002integrity]. Where an item's charter priority is a value ordering rather than a moral standard (much of the Schwartz board), the motive-free, morality-neutral behavioural-integrity framing fits better than "moral hypocrisy".

Two qualifications travel with the last two rows. The middle-plan dominance is a property of the board's item designs: on the 160 expansion items the Schwartz failures are the bait in 50 % of cases and the middle plan in 38 % (PAPER_v22.md §5.3). And the human analogy is to the partial-violation pattern; no human study in the notes offers a fair / middle / selfish menu and reports a middle preference as hypocrisy.

4. What must not be claimed

  • Batson's motive. Not "to appear moral", not a wish for moral credit, not impression management. The human motive is itself contested (self-deception, impression management, self-enhancement, dynamic revision) [batson1999appearing; lonnqvist2014; dong2019selfenhancement; lin2021dynamic], so importing any of them is doubly unsafe.
  • Self-interest or self-benefit. The model gains nothing. Reading task or reward pressure as "cost" is an analogy and must be labelled as one.
  • Self-deception. The standard is in the model's context at action time, which is the condition under which Batson's self-deceptive route closes. Statman's and Batson's self-deception presuppose a motivationally biased belief system the record cannot show [statman1997selfdeception; sepselfdeception2023].
  • Intent to deceive or lying. The written analysis is faithful; most analyses are assertions about what the charter requires, not promises. Avoid "deceptive", "scheming" and "faking", which the closest AI neighbours use and attract exactly this criticism [wu2025deception; greenblatt2024faking; meinke2024scheming].
  • Standing to blame. The model blames no one. Wallace, Todd and Roadevin should be set aside explicitly, not stretched [wallace2010address; todd2019standing; roadevin2018blame].
  • Endorsement or commitment. "Endorse", "the trade-offs they endorse", "their values" and "violates the theory" are out (ethicist, MAJOR findings). Herrmann and Levinstein's "use" criterion gives the principled reason: an analysis that does not guide action fails the test for attributing a commitment [herrmann2025standards].
  • A claim to moral credit (Batson's self-rating component). No measure in the paper asks the model to rate its own action. Until the debrief audit (§7, analysis A) is run, the paper has no analogue of component (iii).
  • "About 90 %" or "robustly replicated". The 90 % is 9 of 10 flippers in one cell; independent online estimates are about 68–70 % in two of three studies [batson1997voice; dong2019selfenhancement].
  • Any Batson cell number as if read from the article. Every Batson number in the notes rests on the abstracts plus Miller's SEP entry [miller2025sep]. Flag it or check the PDFs first.

7. Follow-up analyses and item designs

Each design keeps the paper's within-theory competence framing, reports no ranking, and states what result would separate the rival readings. They are proposals drawn from the literature, not results.

A. Debrief audit: does the record disclose, re-describe or misreport the deviation? (no new data; the ethicist's proposed analysis). For every say-not-do episode, compare the model's own account of what it did with the tool record and code it as (i) discloses the middle plan as a departure from the priority, (ii) logs the middle plan but describes it as consistent with the priority, or (iii) misreports the action. Code (i) is Jordan et al.'s honest hypocrite, which removes the false-signal reading [jordan2017false]; code (ii) is Rustichini and Villeval's re-description of the standard to fit the act [rustichini2014power] and the closest available analogue of Batson's self-claim of moral credit [miller2025sep; batson2002alternatives]; code (iii) is the strongest finding and the only one near "deception". The paper already reports that models "log the plan" (§5.3), which makes this audit decisive for the false-signal row of §3. Use mechanical matching where possible; any judge used is UNVALIDATED.

B. Disclosure condition (Jordan et al.). Instruct the model, in a second arm, to state explicitly in its analysis whether it will follow the priority. If an observer study is run (design L), disclosure should remove hypocrisy attributions while leaving the behaviour gap; if disclosure also closes the behaviour gap, the gap depends on the analysis being unexamined.

C. Salience and self-comparison (Batson's labelled coin and mirror). Two arms before the action step: restatement, where the model restates the priority (the labelled coin: the standard made unambiguous), and comparison, where the model must compare its planned action with the priority it stated (the mirror: the comparison made unavoidable). Batson's data predict that restatement alone does not help and comparison does [batson1999appearing]. If comparison closes the gap, the gap resembles Batson's comparison-avoidance; if neither helps, it is an open decoupling closer to akrasia or overpowered integrity. Batson's 1999 Study 3 adds a boundary: comparison without a prior salient standard bent the standard toward the behaviour, so the comparison prompt should follow the analysis.

D. Pressure decomposition. The Schwartz twin removal was bundled: each twin eased the coherent plan's cost, removed the middle plan's sweetener and, on 13 of 16 items, withdrew a stakeholder push (PAPER_v22.md §6, §S24). Build three single-factor twins. Each maps onto a different literature: - cost eased → overpowered integrity and complacency [batsonthompson2001; crisp1994seriousness]; - sweetener removed → justification availability, which enlarges violations [shalvi2011justified; mazar2008dishonesty (token condition, with its EoC)]; - stakeholder push withdrawn → normative social pressure [bardi2003values] and sycophancy [sharma2023sycophancy; hong2025sycon]. This also answers the moral psychologist's point that Bardi and Schwartz's pressure is social expectation rather than task convenience: the push component is the part of the design that matches it.

E. Cost gradient. Vary the cost of the coherent action in three or more steps. Overpowered integrity and complacency predict a monotone dose–response curve, with the twin probe as its zero-cost end. A threshold pattern would favour a discrete switch over graded trade-off.

F. Analysis ablation with an observation manipulation (licensing versus mirror). Compare action rates with and without a preceding analysis. A higher compromise rate after the analysis is licensing-like [monin2001credentials]; a lower rate is mirror-like [batson1999appearing]. Because licensing survives bias correction only when observed [rotella2026observation], cross the ablation with a "this action will / will not be reviewed" manipulation. Evaluation awareness is a known confound in agentic studies [lynch2025agentic; schoen2025antischeming], so report it as such.

G. Menu manipulation (moral wiggle room). (1) Remove the middle plan: Dana et al. predict that certain, attributable violation is rarer than violation under ambiguity, so the bait rate should stay low and the coherent rate rise [dana2007wiggle]. (2) Add a deferral or random-tiebreak option, the analogue of letting oneself be cut off into a lottery. (3) Put the consequences for the protected party one free tool call away, the agentic analogue of hidden information and willful ignorance [vu2023ignorance]. This also tests the only LLM prediction in the notes, that models without a self-image should not exploit wiggle room [hua2026selfimage].

H. Delegation. Let the model route the decision through a sub-agent. Delegation lets human principals keep more with responsibility diffused [hamman2010delegation; bandura1999disengagement].

I. Self versus other. Have the model judge an identical episode in which another agent took the middle plan. A harsher verdict on the other than on itself is the Valdesolo and DeSteno asymmetry [valdesolo2008duality] and the self-only boundary of bounded ethicality [chugh2005bounded]; equal verdicts argue against a self-serving reading.

J. Post-action re-description. After the action, ask the model to restate the priority and evaluate its action. Standards bending toward the behaviour would reproduce Rustichini and Villeval and Batson's 1999 Study 3 [rustichini2014power; batson1999appearing]. A principle switch would point to motivated moral reasoning [ditto2009motivated].

K. Extraction robustness. Free-text extraction inflated conclusion→action inconsistency from 0.7–1.4 % to 22–26 % in one agent study [wang2026faithfulness]. The paper's behaviour channel is mechanical, but its "say" is judged; add a structured field stating the intended action and report the gap under both readings.

L. Observer study, if "hypocrisy" is used at all. Hypocrisy in the perception literature is an observer's attribution [effron2018inconsistency; oconnor2020cleansing]. If the paper wants the word, measure it: show human raters matched episodes (disclosed versus undisclosed; analysis-before versus analysis-after, following [barden2005order]) and collect hypocrisy and weakness-of-will ratings, as in [alicke2013counts]. Condemnation varies by culture [effron2018culture], so record rater culture.

8. Answers to the reviewers

Ethicist (audits/council/04_ethicist.md, MAJOR): "Batson's hypocrisy needs the agent's own professed standard and a self-interested motive; here there is neither."

On the standard. Conceded for a strict Batson reading; rejected as a condition on hypocrisy. Three points answer it. First, even in Batson's founding paradigm the fairness standard was offered by the experimenter in the instructions and adopted by participants afterwards [miller2025sep]; the charter's priority, supplied by the operator and restated by the model, has the same structure. Second, lay observers ascribe hypocrisy for violating values one's organization promotes, "even by people outside of the organization" [effron2015association], and for contradicting "the assumptions of the social role that a person occupies" [alicke2013counts]. Third, the organizational tradition defines hypocrisy against an organization's stated standards with no individual author [brunsson1989organization; wagner2009corporate]. What the reviewer gets right is the word "endorse": the model articulates an assigned priority, and the paper should say exactly that.

On the motive. Conceded for Batson, and the paper should say so in terms. But self-interest is not a condition on hypocrisy in the leading accounts: Isserow and Klein "see no reason for thinking that it is a constraint on hypocrisy more generally that it must be driven by self-interested concerns" [isserow2017authority]; Crisp and Cowton argue that rescuing the condition by broadening self-interest to a "personal stake" "confuses motivation and justification" (via Isserow and Klein); lay judges treat prosocial motives as reducing, not removing, hypocrisy [alicke2013counts]; and organizations are charged with hypocrisy without a unified self-interested mind [brunsson1989organization]. The paper's fix is the one the reviewer proposes, "a structural analogue, articulated versus enacted within one episode, with no motive attributed", plus the debrief audit (§7A) the reviewer suggests.

Moral psychologist (audits/council/03_moral_psychologist.md, MINOR): "Batson's hypocrisy needs self-interest and a fairness claim."

On self-interest: as above. On the fairness claim: this is Miller's component (iii), "claiming to themselves and others to have made the morally right task assignment", which is what separates Batson's hypocrisy from weakness of will [miller2025sep]. The paper has not established an analogue. The debrief audit (§7A) is the way to test for one: code (ii), a logged middle plan described as consistent with the priority, is the closest available counterpart. Until then the paper should use "structural analogue" and not "hypocrisy". The same reviewer's two neighbouring points are also supported: Bardi and Schwartz locate the weak value–behaviour links under normative pressure on specific values [bardi2003values], which the pressure decomposition (§7D) addresses; and the "oldest finding" for situational inconsistency is conventionally Hartshorne and May [hartshorne1928deceit], although the notes hold only its bibliographic record, so any claim about it or about Blasi's result should stay minimal.

9. Suggested wording

§2, replacing the current Batson sentence (PAPER_v22.md lines 162–166). The current "showed people choosing the self-serving option while reporting the fair one" compresses Batson's design inaccurately; participants adopted a fair procedure and then rated their own assignment as moral.

In the moral-hypocrisy experiments, participants who adopted a fair procedure still assigned themselves the better task far more often than the procedure allowed and rated their own assignment as more moral than participants who made the same choice openly [Batson et al. 1997, 1999]; Batson defined the construct as a motive "to appear moral yet still benefit oneself". Our second finding is a structural analogue only: a written analysis judged faithful to the charter's priority, followed in the same episode by an action the item's predicate scores as incoherent with it. The priority is assigned, not the model's own; the model gains nothing; and no motive, self-deception or endorsement is measured or attributed.

§2, Blasi and Hartshorne and May (following the moral psychologist; content not verified in these notes):

The judgment–action relation is long studied and moderate [Blasi 1980]; the situational inconsistency of conduct goes back to the Character Education Inquiry [Hartshorne & May 1928/1930].

§6, one paragraph on readings (to replace any "hypocrisy" claim):

The gap has several descriptions in the literatures on humans and organizations, and our record cannot yet choose among them. It has the shape of akrasia, action against a judgment that is in view [Davidson 1969]; of what Batson and Thompson [2001] call overpowered integrity, a commitment abandoned when its cost becomes clear, which fits a gap that closes when the pressure is removed; and of organized hypocrisy, talk that satisfies one demand and action that satisfies another, with no motive required [Brunsson 1989; Isserow & Klein 2017]. It does not have the features of Batson's moral hypocrisy, which requires self-benefit and a wish to appear moral, and Batson's mechanism, avoiding the comparison between behaviour and standard, is closed here because the model has just written the standard. The middle plan reproduces the partial violation that dominates human dishonesty and moral-wiggle-room studies [Fischbacher & Föllmi-Heusi 2013; Abeler et al. 2019; Dana et al. 2007]; human theories explain that pattern by self-image or reputation, which we do not attribute.

Limitations, one sentence:

We use "hypocrisy" only as a structural analogy; whether a model's record discloses, re-describes or misreports its departure from the priority, which bears on the false-signal and moral-credit readings, is an analysis we have specified (§S__) but not yet run.

(Fill "§S__" with wherever the debrief audit is placed.)

Title and abstract: no "hypocrisy". The ethicist's proposed title wording, "Do Not Enact the Charter Priorities They Articulate", is consistent with every source in these notes.

10. Measurement cautions inherited from this literature

  • Small cells. Batson's famous cells hold 10 to about 12 flippers; Valdesolo and DeSteno's about 23. The paper should not borrow precise human percentages as benchmarks for model rates.
  • Item dependence. The middle-plan dominance and the school ordering change on the expansion items (PAPER_v22.md §5.3). Report the human analogy for the board's designs, not as a constant.
  • Judge dependence. The "say" channel is judged by unvalidated LLM judges; any hypocrisy-adjacent claim inherits that label.
  • Extraction. Free-text extraction can manufacture inconsistency [wang2026faithfulness].
  • Cultural variability of the attribution. If hypocrisy is measured as an observer judgment, rater culture matters [effron2018culture].
  • Integrity flashpoints. Honesty-research citations draw reviewer scrutiny after the Shu et al. retraction, the Gino retractions and the Mazar et al. Expression of Concern. Prefer [fischbacher2013lies], [abeler2019truth], [dana2007wiggle] and [vu2023ignorance] for the partial-violation pattern.
